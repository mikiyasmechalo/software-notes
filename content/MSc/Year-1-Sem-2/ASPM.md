### What is Project Management?
Project management is “the application of knowledge, skills, tools and techniques to project activities to meet project requirements" (PMBOK 5th ed. 2013)
PM is the discipline or art of planning, organizing, and managing resources to bring about the successful completion of specific project goals and objectives.
Project managers strive to meet the triple constraint by balancing project scope, time, and cost goals.

**Project Management Process Groups** 
Regardless of the type of project lifecycle, project management encompasses the following process groups, shown with some representative tasks: 
1. Initiating/Define - Scope the project; Charter the project; identify stakeholders 
2. Planning - Develop the project plan. Collect requirements; identify schedule; plan scope, cost,  quality, human resource, risk, and procurement management 
3. Executing - Launch the plan. Direct and manage project work; perform quality assurance; manage and develop project team; conduct procurements 
4. Monitoring and Controlling - Monitor project progress. Monitor and control project work; manage scope change; monitor and control schedule; control quality; control risks; control procurements.
5. Closing -  Close out the project: Close project; close procurements

### Project Life Cycle
What? a collection of sequential or overlapping project phases.
Phases within the project are defined by technical information transfer or technical component hand-off. e.g. releases in Agile life cycles

*Project Phase?* The completion and approval of one or more deliverables defines its end.
D/t phases can have different relationships among themselves, even within the same project: 
- *Sequential relationship*: A phase starts only when the previous phase is complete. 
- *Overlapping relationship*: A new phase is planned & started before prev. is complete.

**PMBOK PLC vs. SDLC** 
In a *predictive life cycle*: 
- Product and deliverables are deﬁned at the beginning of the project. 
- Changes to scope are carefully and restrictively managed

In an iterative *and incremental life cycle*: 
- Phases repeat one or more project activities, w/c increases understanding of the product. 
- Each phase (and each iteration) successively adds to the functionality of the product. 
- Scope is usually well-deﬁned early in the project life cycle, but can be changed with relatively low overhead as project proceeds. 

In an *adaptive life cycle* (Agile): 
- Product is developed over multiple phases, each with several iterations.
- Detailed scope is deﬁned for each phase only as the phase begins and proceeds.

IT Project Life Cycles

- **SDLC** is directed toward achieving product requirements

Both life cycle models are needed to manage an IT project

##### A. Waterfall
The primary software project phases:
- Requirements identification
- System Analysis
- System Design
- System building (implementation/Construction)

- Deployment and maintenance

Failure symptoms:
- Protracted integration and late design breakage
- Late risk resolution
- Requirements-driven functional decomposition
- Adversarial stakeholder relationships
- Focus on documents and review meetings

Suitable for projects with:
- Clear, unambiguous, and stable user requirements
- Familiar, proven technology
- Low complexity
- Adequate time
- Stable schedule

##### B. Evolutionary (Iterative) Methodologies
An evolutionary methodology follows an iterative and incremental approach that allows the start of development with incomplete, imperfect knowledge.
Advantages: 
- Logical progress toward evolving a robust architecture 
- Effective management of changing requirements 
- Effective means to address changes in planning
- Ability to perform continuous integration
- Early understanding of the system (the ‘Hello world!’ effect)
- Ongoing risk assessment

Incorporates top five principles of modern development processes:
- *Architecture first*. Provides the central design element
- *Iterative life-cycle process*. Provides the essential risk management element
- *Component-based development*. Provides the technology element
- *Change management environment*. Provides the control element
- *Round-trip engineering*. Provides the automation element

Iterative SD model defines four life-cycle phases:
- **Inception** - formulate scope, feasibility analysis, synthesize sys. arch. (constraints & trade-offs), plan and prepare business case (risk mgt., staffing, iteration plans, cost and infra.)
- **Elaboration** - Most critical, - elaborate vision, elaborate process and infra, elaborate arch. and select reusable components.
	- Baseline the architecture as quickly as possible and demonstrate that the architecture will support the vision at reasonable cost in reasonable time.
- **Construction** - achieve useful versions (alpha, beta..), perform resource mgt., control and process mgt. , complete component development and cost, assess product releases
- **Transition** - Perform deployment-specific engineering tasks. Assess deployment baselines against complete vision and acceptance criteria. plan for next iteration.

Suitable for projects with:
- Reasonably-but not perfectly-clear user requirements 
- Unfamiliar or unproven technology 
- High complexity 
- Short time schedule 
- Schedule variability

#####  C. Overview of Agile Projects
Agile is an umbrella term used for identifying various models, such as Scrum. 
The Agile Manifesto consists of 4 key values such as: 
- Individuals and interactions over processes and tools.

The 5 C's of agile commonly refer to: *collaboration*, *communication*, *customer focus*, *continuous improvement*, and *change readiness*. behaviors and mindset teams need to succeed in agile environments.

The Agile Manifesto consists of 4 key values and 12 principles such as:
- *Customer Satisfaction*: early and continuous delivery of valuable software.
- *Welcome Change*: Changing requirements are welcomed, even late in development.
- *Frequent Delivery*: Deliver working software frequently, in shorter timescales (weeks).
- *Daily Collaboration*: Business people and developers work together daily.
- *Motivated Individuals*: Build projects around motivated individuals and trust them.
- *Face-to-Face Conversation*: The most efficient method of conveying information.
- *Working Software*: The primary measure of progress.
- *Sustainable Development*: Maintain a constant, sustainable pace.
- *Technical Excellence*: Continuous attention to good design and quality.
- *Simplicity*: The art of maximizing the amount of work not done is essential.
- *Self-Organizing Teams*: Best architectures from self-organizing teams.
- *Regular Reflection*: on how to become more effective and adjust accordingly. 

An agile project is completed in small sections called iterations, or, in scrum, sprints.
Insights gained from the critique of an iteration are used to determine what the next step should be in the project.
Iterations usually last 2 weeks.

**Agile Project Frameworks and Steps** 
Some of the major agile steps include:
1. The product owner identifies the *product vision*. 
2. The product owner creates a *product roadmap and release plan*. 
3. The product owner, the (scrum) master, and the development team *plan sprints*, also called iterations, and start creating the product within those sprints.
4. During each sprint, the development team has *daily meetings*.
5. The team holds a *sprint review*.
6. The team holds a *sprint retrospective*.

**Agile Project Artifacts** 
1. *Product vision statement*: An elevator pitch, to communicate how your product supports the company's or organization's strategies. Revisit once a year.
2. *Product roadmap*: The product roadmap is a high-level view of the product requirements, with a loose time frame for when you will develop those requirements. Revisit twice a year.
3. *Release plan*: A high-level timetable for the release of working software.
4. *Product backlog*: The full list of what is in the scope for your project, ordered by priority. Once you have your first requirement, you have a product backlog.
5. *Sprint backlog*: The goal, user stories, and tasks associated with the current sprint.
6. *Increment*: The working product functionality at the end of each sprint.

**Agile Project Roles** 
1. *Development team*: Programmers, testers, designers, writers ...
2. *Product owner*: responsible for bridging the gap between the customer, B. stakeholders, and the development team. Sometimes called a customer representative.
3. *Scrum master*: responsible for supporting the development team, clearing organizational roadblocks, and keeping the agile process consistent. sometimes called a project facilitator.
4. *Stakeholders*: Anyone with an interest in the project.
5. *Agile mentor*: Someone who has experience implementing agile projects.
	- Provides valuable feedback and advice to new project teams and to project teams that want to perform at a higher level.

**Agile Project Events** 
1. *Project planning*: creating a product vision statement and a product roadmap can take place in as little time as one day.
2. *Release planning*: Planning the next set of product features to release.
3. *Sprint*: A short cycle of dev., in which the team creates potentially shippable P. functionality. 
4. *Sprint planning*: meeting at the beginning of each sprint where the team commits to a goal.
5. *Daily scrum*: A 15-minute meeting held each day, where the team states what they did, will do and whether they have any roadblocks.
6. *Sprint review*: A meeting where the development team demonstrates the working product functionality it completed during the sprint. 
7. *Sprint retrospective*: A meeting where the scrum team discusses what went well, what could change, and how to make any changes.

##### Planning Process
The project planning phase focuses on integrating sub-plans to create a single source of truth.
1. Scope & Activities
	* **Scope Planning & Definition:** Decide how to define the project and then create the formal **Scope Statement**.
	* **Work Breakdown Structure (WBS):** Decompose the project into manageable work packages.
	* **Activity Management:** Define specific activities, sequence them, and estimate the resources and duration needed for each.
2. Schedule & Resources
	* **Schedule Development:** Analyze activity outputs to create the **Project Network** and final schedule.
	* **Resource Planning:** Identify and secure the necessary personnel, materials, and equipment.
3. Financials & Quality
	* **Cost Management:** Estimate costs for individual tasks and aggregate them into a **Cost Baseline** (budgeting).
	* **Quality Planning:** Identify relevant standards and determine how to meet them.
4. Support Plans
	* **HR & Communications:** Plan team roles and how information will be shared.
	* **Risk Management:** Identify risks, analyze their impact/probability (Qualitative & Quantitative), and plan responses to mitigate them.
	* **Procurement:** Plan what to buy, how to contract it, and prepare the final project proposal.

### What is a Project Charter?
AKA *project definition*, or *project statement* is a statement of the scope, objectives, and participants in a project.
Project definition starts with the *Conditions of Satisfaction document* based on conversation with the customer. charter is generated from the Conditions of Satisfaction document.
Often precedes *Statement of Work*, often 2-4 pages (can be longer)

It provides a preliminary: 
- delineation of roles and responsibilities, 
- outlines the project objectives, 
- identifies the main stakeholders, and 
- defines the authority of the project manager.

**WHY?** 
Having a project charter provides your project with the following benefits: 
- Formally authorizes the project
- Creates a vision and a shared understanding about the project
- Empowers the project manager to lead the project
- Identifies the high-level objectives and scope of the project
- Defines what will success look like
- Enables support for the project to be gained
- Ensures that key stakeholders are aware of the project
- Secures budget and resources for the project
- Serves as a point of reference for the project team

**What Should PC Contain?** 
- *Project Information*: ID and name of the project, name of the PM, and project sponsor.
- *Business need, problem or opportunity*: (justiﬁcation) what is the main driver for this project to exist?
- *Project Objectives and Benefits*: (success criteria) what are you trying to achieve with the project? Try writing your objectives as SMART.
- *Project Organization*: describes the governance of the project, including key roles for its management and direction.
- *High-level project scope*: i.e., what will be in scope and out of the scope of the project, as well as key deliverables to be created by the project.
- *High-level project timescale*: list the key stages and estimated duration for the project, as well as its key milestones.
- *High-level project budget*: to identify the budget requirements of the project, including capital and revenue expenditure forecast.
- *Key assumptions*: main assumptions that may impact the project if proven false
- *Key project risks*: main risks that may impact the project if they materialize.
- *Success criteria*: key measures to assess the success of the project

**Best Practices to Write a Project Charter** 
- *Keep it brief*: keep your Project Charter simple and brief.
- *Be explicit*: Get to the point as early as possible and make it explicit.
- *Build it with your sponsor*: The involvement of the sponsor is fundamental.
- *Get it signed-off*: until you get the Charter to be signed-off by your sponsor, the project is not formally authorized.

##### The Work Breakdown Structure 
A hierarchical description of the work that must be done to complete the project as defined in the Project Overview Statement (POS).
- **Activity**: An activity is simply a chunk of work. 
* **Task**: A task is a smaller chunk of work. - basic element.
* **Milestone**: a task of zero duration. Usually an external event. Can be used to mark completion of an activity or phase.

Tasks are *decomposed* into more manageable components called **work packages** (lowest level).
Reliable time, cost, and resource estimates can be made at the level of work packages in a project.

‘Work’ in the context of the WBS means work products or deliverables, not the effort itself, as in ‘staff-hours of effort’

The level of detail for work packages vary depending on project size and complexity.
For IT projects using the agile process we have defined, each iteration (sprint) defines one or more work package.

The WBS is: 
- *Structured hierarchically*: each successively lower level represents a more-detailed deﬁnition of project work. 
- *Deliverable-oriented*: each lower level represents a more detailed deﬁnition of project work. 
- *A representation of project scope*: it organizes and deﬁnes the total scope of the project.

Work packages are scheduled, cost estimated, monitored, and controlled.

Each item in the WBS has both a unique identifier (the code or account identifier) and a WBS code.
The ID does not change if moved but WBS code might (if moved).

The *just right principle* states that no more decomposition is needed–it would be too detailed–and any less decomposition would be too coarse.
- Insufficient decomposition leads to reduced ability to plan, manage, and control the work.
- Excessive decomposition leads to wasted effort and decreased efficiency.

**Uses for the WBS** 
* *Thought process tool:* Helps the project manager and team visualize how to define and manage the project work effectively.
* *Architectural design tool:* Provides a visual picture of the project work and demonstrates how individual work items relate to one another.
* *Planning tool:* Breaks the project down into detailed activities to estimate effort, time, and resources, and to build the schedule at the lowest work level.
* *Project status reporting tool:* Structures status reporting by consolidating lower-level activity completion into higher-level milestones for management and customers.

**Six Criteria to Test for Completeness in the WBS** 
1. Status/completion is measurable
2. Start/end events are clearly defined
3. Activity has a deliverable
4. Time/cost is easily estimated
5. Activity duration is within acceptable limits
6. Work assignments are independent

### Project Time Management
Concerned with analyzing the logical and temporal relationships among the activities needed to complete the project.
**Three elements** 
- The `schedule data` and associated calculations (e.g., activity deﬁnitions and estimates).
- The `scheduling method` applied to the schedule data in order to deﬁne estimated start and end dates for activities and milestones, including project completion.
- The `schedule` that represents the output from a scheduling tool applying the method to the data.

**Processes** 
1. **Define activities:** Identifies specific tasks needed to complete work packages.
2. **Sequence activities:** Identifies and documents relationships between activities.
3. **Estimate activity resources:** Estimates the types and amounts of resources required.
4. **Estimate activity durations:** Estimates the time periods needed to complete activities.
5. **Develop schedule:** Analyzes sequences, durations, and resources to create the timeline.
6. **Control schedule:** Monitors progress and manages updates to the schedule.

**Planning, Estimating, Scheduling**
- *Plan*: Identify activities. No specific start and end dates.
- *Estimating*: Determining the size & duration of activities.
- *Schedule*: Adds specific start and end dates, relationships, and resources.

**How to schedule** 
* **Identify "what" needs to be done:** Create a Work Breakdown Structure (WBS) to break down project scope.
* **Identify "how much" (size):** Use size estimation techniques to determine the scale of the work.
* **Identify duration:** Apply effort estimation techniques to figure out how long tasks will take.
* **Identify task dependencies:** Map out relationships using dependency graphs, network diagrams, Gantt charts, or PERT.
* **Estimate total duration:** Formulate the actual schedule using techniques like PERT and Critical Path Method (CPM).

**Overview Activity Scheduling** 
"Define activities" Identiﬁes the speciﬁc actions or tasks needed to carry out the project and produce the project deliverables.
"Create WBS" - traditionally defines the project deliverables and work packages.
- in adaptive/agile - only the high-level deliverables are deﬁned up front, while work packages are deﬁned at the iteration level.

Recall that: 
- Deliverables and work packages are the planning units for project management purposes.
- Tasks (activities) are the planning unit for development purposes.
- An iteration/sprint comprises one or more work packages (and their associated activities). 

**Sidebar: A Useful Rule of Thumb** 
No work package should have a duration greater than four to six weeks.
For knowledge work, durations should be in the range of one to three weeks, because knowledge work is harder to track than tangible work.
- People tend to back-end load tasks with lengthy durations by pushing all the effort toward the end.


##### Agile Perspective: Deﬁne Activities
PMBOK states: “The activity list is a comprehensive list that includes all schedule activities required on the project."
In an adaptive/agile project, this list cannot and should not be generated early in the project. if not it leads to wasted effort and inconsistencies between documents and reality.
Instead, agile method delays speciﬁc activity deﬁnition to the start of each individual sprint, and delegates the activity deﬁnition to the individual sprint development teams.

### Activity Sequencing
It identifies and documents the logical relationships among activities in a project.
Activity sequencing, though executed differently is an important concept in all project management methodologies.

**Inputs** - Scope statement
**Outputs** - project schedule network diagrams, Updated activity list/WBS

##### Project Schedule Network Diagrams

*Arrow Diagramming Method (ADM):* 
* Represents activities as **arrows** and connects them using nodes (circles) to mark start and end points.
* Supports only Finish-to-Start relationships.
* Requires "dummy activities" (dashed arrows) to show certain logical connections without adding time or cost.

*Precedence Diagramming Method (PDM):* 
* Represents activities as **boxes (nodes)** and uses arrows to show the dependencies between them.
* Supports four relationship types: Finish-to-Start, Start-to-Start, Finish-to-Finish, and Start-to-Finish.
* Eliminates the need for dummy activities and easily incorporates leads and lags.

**Dependency Type** 
* **Finish-to-start (FS):** The successor activity cannot start until the predecessor activity finishes (e.g., testing begins only after coding is completed).
* **Finish-to-finish (FF):** The successor activity cannot finish until the predecessor activity finishes (e.g., final component acceptance completes only when the last subcomponent acceptance completes).
* **Start-to-start (SS):** The successor activity cannot start until the predecessor activity starts (e.g., acquiring third-party software triggers the start of developer training).
* **Start-to-finish (SF):** The successor activity cannot finish until the predecessor activity starts (e.g., a legacy system cannot be shut down until the new system goes live).
* **Percent complete:** The final portion ($n\%$) of a successor activity depends on a specific progress milestone ($m\%$) of the predecessor activity (e.g., the last 30% of interface testing begins when application development hits 50%).

**Dependency Type Determination** 
* **Mandatory dependencies:** Limitations inherent in the nature of the work that cannot be avoided (e.g., code development must be complete before acceptance testing begins).
* **Discretionary dependencies:** Non-inherent relationships established by the project team based on best practices, preferences, or risk mitigation (e.g., scheduling high-risk activities early).
* **Internal dependencies:** Relationships between activities that are completely within the control of the project team (e.g., triggering developer training as soon as a third-party component procurement starts).
* **External dependencies:** Links between project tasks and non-project activities that are typically outside the project team's control (e.g., waiting for a third-party vendor to deliver a custom component).

### Duration
The duration of a project is the elapsed time in business working days, not including weekends, holidays, or other nonworking days.
Duration is different from work effort. 
- Work effort is labor required to complete an activity. That labor can be consecutive or nonconsecutive hours.
- Duration is work effort divided by number of people doing the work;
- Or, work effort is the duration multiplied by the number of people doing the work.
- E.g. five people reviewing a document; the duration may be 10 hours but the work effort is 50 staff-hours

- The duration of the activity is the clock time or “Elapsed time” that we want to estimate.
- Productivity is usually 50-75% of a business day

##### Causes of Variation
*Causes of Variation in the Actual Activity Duration* 
- **Varying skill levels**. Estimations are done based on average skilled engineer but the actual may be higher or lower.
- **Unexpected events**. Random acts of nature, vendor delays, traffic jams, power failures, etc.
- **Efficiency of work time**. Every time a worker is interrupted it takes more time to get up to the level of productivity prior to the time of the interruption. 
- **Mistakes and Misunderstandings**. In organizations that have a documented process, rework may have its toll on the actual activity duration.

*   **Expert Judgment:** Highly effective, often used with other tools.

##### Network Analysis
Network analysis is the technique that generates the project schedule.
Network analysis may use several different analysis methods to calculate the early and late start dates for project activities.

 These methods may be combined and include:
- Gantt Charts
- Critical Path Method (CPM)
- Critical Chain Method (CCM)
- What-if analysis
- Resource leveling

The Precedence Diagram Method (PDM) is a graphical network technique that establishes activity sequencing.

Network analysis may also make use of mandatory or discretionary parallelism in project activities to allow schedule compression.
Here's a shortened version of the terminology:

**Terminology** 
*   **Activity:** A task that consumes time and potentially resources.
*   **Critical (Activity/Event):** Must be completed by a specific time with no flexibility (no slack).
*   **Critical Path:** The longest sequence of activities in a project, determining the earliest project completion. Any delay on this path delays the project end.
*   **Events:** Specific points in time marking the start or end of activities.
*   **Milestone:** A significant event in a project, often marking the completion of a major phase. Project reviews are often conducted at milestones.


**Creating a Precedence Table** 
A precedence table outlines task durations and their interdependencies.
- **Estimating Task Duration:**
	- Base estimates on historical data or expert knowledge (often from task leaders/functional managers).
	- Include contingency factors for risky tasks (technology, skills, dependencies, resource shortages).

- **Determining Task Interdependencies:**
	- Identify predecessor/successor relationships to properly order tasks.
	- Look for truly independent parallel tasks to potentially shorten the schedule, minimizing backtracking risk.
	- This should be a collaborative team effort to prevent issues.

### Project Time Management II

##### Critical Path Method (CPM) 
- a networking diagraming technique used to predict total project duration.
- A *Critical Path* is the series of activities that determine the earliest time by which the project can be completed,
	- it is the longest path through the network diagram and has the least amount of slack or float.

**Calculating the Critical path** 
First, Develop a good network diagram and add the duration estimates for all activities on each path through the network diagram
- Longest path is the critical path.

If one or more of the activities on the critical path takes longer than planned, the whole project schedule will slip unless the project manager takes corrective action.

A **forward pass** analysis performs schedule calculations that identify the *early start* and *ﬁnish* dates of activities and the project.
A **backward pass** analysis performs schedule calculations that identify the *late start* and *ﬁnish* dates of activities and the project, as well as total and free ﬂoat.

- **Total ﬂoat (TF)** is the amount of time an activity can be delayed without delaying the project as a whole.
- **Free ﬂoat (FF)** is the amount of time an activity can be delayed without delaying its successor (dependent) activities.

##### Gantt Chart
A Gantt Chart (named for Henry Laurence Gantt) consists of a table of project task information and a bar chart that graphically displays project schedule, depicting progress in relation to time and often used in planning and tracking a project.
- Horizontal bar chart format, with bars representing the phases and activities of the WBS.
- Time extends along the horizontal axis.
- Able to show planned and actual progress on tasks as well as task dependencies
- Effective communication tool but with limitations
- Very low information density: lots of wasted space
- Not very useful for large projects

**Deﬁnitions** 
- *Risk* is the probability of incurring some net loss while pursuing a goal (opportunity).
- *A reducible risk* is one which is predictable or within our control: we can reduce the likelihood of loss by taking steps to mitigate or avoid the risk.
- *Irreducible* risks are more difﬁcult to deal with; may be `unpredictable` or `Beyond our control`.

**Risk management** is a systematic approach to reducing the harm due to risks, making a project less vulnerable to challenge or failure (e.g., cost or schedule overruns, scope decrease, quality reduction) and its resulting product more robust. 

PMBOK Guide:
- “Project risk is an uncertain event or condition that, if it occurs, has a positive or negative impact on at least one project objective, such as time, cost, scope, or quality."

“A risk may have one or more causes, and, if it occurs, one or more impacts.”

- What can go wrong?
- What is the likelihood?
- What will the damage be?
- What can we do about it?

**How to Categorize Risk** 
*Known, unknown, unknowable* 

*Known Risks* - can be uncovered after careful evaluation of the project plan, business and technical environment and other sources of information.
- can be estimated from historical information
- can be mitigated by mgt techs and response plans.
e.g. delay in delivery from third-party-vendor, personnel leaving the project.

*Predictable Risks* (but unknown risks): Risks that can be extrapolated from past projects.  (Staff turnover, poor communication with the customer)
- Risks that we know have a probability of occurring, but do not know the precise impact
- Cannot be managed directly but can be mitigated by the use of contingency
e.g. Loss of key personnel due to turnover

*Unpredictable Risks* “Joker” risks that are hard to predict.

*Unknowable risks* are outside the scope of historical or probabilistic models for the project
- Are beyond the scope of risk management and usually are addressed by crisis or disaster management
e.g. Corporate failures, natural disasters, acts of terrorism or war, major snowstorm and power loss

##### Qualitative Risk Analysis
Concerned with prioritizing identified risks.
Priority is determined by estimating a risk’s probability of occurrence and its impact on the project.
Helps determine if it is necessary to perform `quantitative risk analysis`, a more complex analysis process.

**Inputs** 
- Organizational process assets
- Project scope statement
- Risk management plan
- Risk register

**Risk Analysis** 
Risk Exposure (RE)
- a.k.a. “Risk Impact”
- RE = Probability of loss * size of loss
- Ex: risk is “Facilities not ready on time”
	- Probability is 25%, size is 4 weeks, RE is 1 week
- Ex: risk is “Inadequate design - redesign required”
	- Probability is 15%, size is 10 weeks, RE is 1.5 weeks
- Statistically are “expected values”
- Sum all RE’s to get expected overrun
- Which is pre risk management

*Estimating probability of loss* 
- Use team member estimates and have a risk-estimate review
- Use Delphi or group-consensus techniques
- Use gambling analogy “how much would you bet”
- Use “adjective calibration”: highly likely, probably, improbable, unlikely, highly unlikely

**Probability and Impact Matrix** 
Defines a combination of risk probability and risk impact that helps determine which risks need detailed risk response plans.
Matrix is typically defined by the organization, if not developed during planning meeting.

*Quantifying risk probability* 
- subjective or numeric
- For most situations, use of a ﬁve-point Likert scale is appropriate: 
	- Highly unlikely (p < 20%) 
	- Unlikely (20% < p < 40%) 
	- About even (40% < p < 60%) 
	- Likely (60% < p < 80%) 
	- Highly likely (p > 80%) 
- For less well-deﬁned situations, use a three-point scale: 
	- High (p > 75%) 
	- Moderate (35% < p < 75%) 
	- Low (p < 35%) 

*Impact* 
is the amount of pain or gain the risk event poses to the various project objectives: cost, time, scope, and quality.
- can be subjective (low, med, high) or numeric

**Risk Prioritization** 
One way to prioritize risks is to estimate the probability of its occurrence and its consequences (loss) when it does occur.
The expected value of the loss for the risk can be used for prioritization. This expected value is called `risk exposure`.
If $P_r$ is the probability of a risk R occurring and $L_r$ is the total loss incurred if the risk materializes, then Risk exposure RE is
 $$RE_r=P_r*L_r$$ 
**Probability and impact matrix** 
A single value to characterize the combined effects of these two risk influences: **the risk rating**.
Probability and impact matrix assigns an overall risk rating to each risk.
The combination of probability and impact results in an ordinal (order-based) risk rating usually expressed as low, medium, or high.
The PMBOK Guide also assigns a color code to each rating: low risks are green, medium risks are yellow, and high risks are red,
