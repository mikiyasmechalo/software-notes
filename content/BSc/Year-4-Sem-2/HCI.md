# CH 1: What is Interaction Design
**What is interaction design?** 
“Designing interactive products to support the way people communicate and interact in their everyday and working lives.” Sharp, Rogers, and Preece (2019)
“The design of spaces for human communication and interaction.” Winograd (1997)

Interaction design is the umbrella term covering all of these aspects:
- Fundamental to all disciplines, fields, and approaches concerned with researching and designing computer-based systems for people 

Academic disciplines contributing to ID: 
- Psychology
- Social Sciences
- Computing Sciences
- Engineering
- Ergonomics
- Informatics 
- Graphic design
- Product design
- Artist-design
- Industrial design
- Film industry

**What to design** 
Need to take into account:
- Who the users are
- What activities are being carried out
- Where interaction is taking place

**Working in multidisciplinary teams** 
Benefits
- More ideas and designs generated
Disadvantages
- Difficult to communicate and progress forward the designs being created

*The user experience* 
The way people feel about it and their pleasure and satisfaction when using it, looking at it, holding it, and opening or closing it.
Every product that is used by someone has a user experience.

> "Cannot design a user experience−only can design for a user experience"
>Designers can create the elements of a product (like buttons, menus, and flow), but they can't control how each individual user actually feels or perceives their interaction with it. The user's personal background, context, and expectations all shape their unique experience. Designers aim to create the best possible conditions *for* a good experience, but they don't design the experience itself.

*User experience* - How users perceive a product, such as whether a smartwatch is seen as sleek or chunky, and their emotional reaction to it, such as whether people have a positive experience when using it.

*Hassenzahl’s (2010) model of the user experience* 
- *Pragmatic*: how simple, practical, and obvious it is for the user to achieve their goals
- *Hedonic*: how evocative and stimulating the interaction is to users 

**Core characteristics of interaction design** 
- Users should be involved throughout the development of the project
- Specific usability and user experience goals need to be identified, clearly documented, and agreed to at the beginning of the project
- Iteration is needed through the core activities

**Accessibility and inclusiveness** 
*Accessibility*: primarily concerned with removing barriers that prevent people with disabilities from using a product or service. It's about ensuring equal access.
- Focus is on people with disabilities; e.g., `VoiceOver` for visually impaired.
*Inclusiveness*: a broader concept that aims to design products and services that cater to the widest possible range of human diversity. designs products for everyone, regardless of disability, age, income, etc. 
- For example, affordable, easy-to-use smartphones

**Disabilities** 
Disabilities can be classified as:
- *Sensory impairment* (such as loss of vision or hearing)
- *Physical impairment* (having loss of functions to one or more parts of the body after a stroke or spinal cord injury)
- *Cognitive* (including learning impairment or loss of memory/cognitive function due to old age)

**Usability goals** 
- Effective to use
- Efficient to use
- Safe to use
- Have good utility
- Easy to learn
- Easy to remember how to use

### Design principles
- Generalizable abstractions for thinking about different aspects of design
- The do’s and don'ts of interaction design
- Derived from a mix of theory-based knowledge, experience, and common-sense

**Constraints** 
- Restricting the possible actions that can be performed
- Helps prevent user from selecting incorrect options
 
**Consistency** 
Design interfaces to have similar operations and use similar elements for similar tasks.

*Internal and external consistency* 
Internal consistency refers to designing operations to behave the same within an application
- Difficult to achieve with complex interfaces

External consistency refers to designing operations, interfaces, and so on to be the same across applications and devices
- Very rarely the case, based on different designer’s preference

**Affordances: to give a clue** 
Refers to an attribute of an object that allows people to know how to use it.
- (For example, a mouse button invites pushing, a door handle affords pulling)

- Interfaces are virtual and do not have affordances like physical objects.
- Instead, interfaces are better conceptualized as ‘perceived’ affordances not 'real',
	- Learned conventions of arbitrary mappings between action and effect at the interface
	- Some mappings are better than others

It is a process:
- Focused on discovering requirements, designing to fulfil requirements, producing prototypes and evaluating them
- Involves trade-offs to balance conflicting requirements

Four approaches: *user-centered design*, *activity-centered design*, *systems design*, and *genius design*.

- *Early focus on users and tasks*: directly studying cognitive, behavioral, anthropomorphic, and attitudinal characteristics 
- *Empirical measurement*: users’ reactions and performance to scenarios, manuals, simulations, and prototypes are observed, recorded, and analyzed
- *Iterative design*: when problems are found in user testing, fix them and carry out more tests

# CH 3: CONCEPTUALIZING INTERACTION DESIGN?
### Conceptualizing design
**Why the need to conceptualizing design?** 
- To scrutinize vague ideas and assumptions about the benefits of the proposed product in terms of their feasibility
- How realistic is it to develop?
- How desirable and useful?

**Assumptions and claims** 
*Assumption* - is something that is taken for granted or accepted as true *without explicit proof, statement, or justification*, even when it might require further investigation. - implicit
- e.g. "people will want to watch TV while driving." - takes for granted that users will *desire* this functionality. with no evidence.

*Claim* - is a statement asserting something to be true, often based on some form of evidence or reasoning, but which is *still open to question, debate, or requires further*. - explicit
- statements that are meant to persuade or convince the audience.
- e.g. 'a multimodal style of interaction for controlling GPS, one that involves speaking while driving is safe.'" states something as true ("is safe") but this truth is "still open to question."

Write down your assumptions and claims when coming up with a new design.
Those that are difficult to articulate 
- Can highlight what ideas are vague or unrealistic 
- Identify human activities and interactivities that are problematic 

e.g. What were the assumptions and claims made about watching 3D TV?
*An assumption* 
- People would really enjoy the enhanced clarity and color detail provided by 3D 
*A claim* 
- People would not mind paying a lot more for a new 3D-enabled TV screen because of the new experience


### Benefits of conceptualizing
**Orientation**  
- Enables design teams to ask specific questions about how the conceptual model will be understood
**Open-minded**  
- Prevents design teams from becoming narrowly focused early on
**Common ground** 
- Allows design teams to establish a set of commonly agreed terms

*From problem space to design space* 
Having a good understanding of the problem space can help inform the design space
- For example, what kind of interface, behavior, functionality to provide
Before deciding upon these, it is important to develop a conceptual model

##### Conceptual model 
A conceptual model is: “…a high-level description of how a system is organized and operates”.
A conceptual model enables: “…designers to straighten out their thinking before they start laying out their widgets” (Johnson and Henderson, 2002, p28) 

Provides a working strategy and framework of general concepts and their interrelations,

**Components of a conceptual model** 
- Metaphors and analogies - Understand what a product is for and how to use it for an activity
- Concepts that people are exposed to through the product - Task–Domain objects, their attributes, and operations 
- Relationship and mappings between these concepts 

**First steps in formulating a conceptual model** 
- What will the users be doing when carrying out their tasks?
- How will the system support these?
- What kind of interface metaphor, if any, will be appropriate?
- What kinds of interaction modes and styles to use? 

**Conceptual models** 
The best conceptual models are often those that appear: 
- Obvious and simple
- The operations they support are intuitive to use

**Interface metaphors** 
Interface designed to be similar to a physical entity but also has own properties
- For example, desktop metaphor, and web portals

Based on activity, object, or a combination of both
Exploit user’s familiar knowledge, helping them to understand ‘the unfamiliar’ 

*Examples* 
- Conceptualizing what users are doing - "surfing the Web"
- A conceptual model instantiated at the interface - the desktop metaphor
- Visualizing an operation - an icon of a shopping cart into which the user places items

*Benefits of interface metaphors* 
- Makes learning new systems easier
- Helps users understand the underlying conceptual model
- Can be very innovative and enable accessibility to a greater diversity of users

**Problems with Interface Metaphors:** 
1. **Breaks conventions**: Using metaphors can lead to unconventional interface design, such as placing a recycle bin on the desktop.
2. **Constrains designers**: Metaphors can limit designers' creativity and force them to think within the boundaries of the chosen metaphor.
3. **Conflicts with design principles**: Metaphors can be at odds with established design principles, leading to poor user experience.
4. **Limited understanding**: Users are forced to understand the system solely through the lens of the metaphor, rather than having a clear understanding of the underlying functionality.
5. **Transfer of bad design**: Designers may inadvertently reuse bad design elements from existing metaphors, perpetuating poor design.
6. **Limited imagination**: Metaphors can stifle designers' ability to come up with new and innovative conceptual models.


### Interaction types
* **Instructing**: Issuing commands and selecting options. e.g. Tell the time, print a file, or save a file
	* Very common
	* it's main benefit is that it supports quick and efficient interactions, good for repetition
* **Conversing**: Interacting with a system as if having a conversation
	* Ranges from simple voice recognition menu-driven systems to more complex ‘natural language’ dialogs
	* e.g. search engines, advice-giving systems, and help systems, also virtual agents, chatbots, toys, and pet robots
	* Pros: familiar to everyone (comfortable)
	* Cons: Misunderstandings when the system doesn't understand 
* **Manipulating**: Interacting with objects in a virtual or physical space
	* dragging, selecting, opening, closing and zooming actions on virtual objects
	* Exploit’s users’ knowledge of how they move and manipulate in the physical world
	* can be using physical controls or air gestures
	* "*Direct Manipulation* (DM)" - coined by `Ben Shneiderman` (1983) , 3 Core properties
		* Continuous representation of objects and actions of interest
		- Physical actions and button pressing instead of issuing commands with complex syntax
		- Rapid reversible actions with immediate feedback on object of interest
	- Benefits of DM
		- Novices can learn the basic functionality quickly while Experienced users can work extremely rapidly
		- Intermittent users can retain operational concepts over time
		- Error messages rarely needed
		- Quick responses to actions, less anxiety for users and users feel confidence & control
	- Disadvantages of DM
		- Some people take the metaphor of direct manipulation too literally
		- Not all tasks can be described by objects, and not all actions can be done directly
		- Moving a cursor using a mouse or touchpad can be slower than pressing function keys to do the same actions
* **Exploring**: Moving through a virtual environment or a physical space
	* Physical environments can also be embedded with sensors that when detect the presence of someone will trigger digital or physical events to happen
	* e.g. virtual environments of Cities, parks, datasets...
* **Responding**: The system initiates the interaction and the user chooses whether to respond
	* System does this by detecting the location and-or presence of someone in a vicinity and notifies them on their phone or watch, 
	* e.g. Alerts the user of a nearby coffee bar where some friends are meeting
	* e.g. fitness tracker notifying user when reaching a milestone
	* Potential Cons: 
		* Can get tiresome or frustrating if too many
		* What does it do when it gets something wrong? apologize? be corrected?


**Choosing an interaction type** 
- Direct manipulation is good for ‘doing’ types of tasks, for example, designing, drawing, flying, driving, or sizing windows
- Issuing instructions is good for repetitive tasks, for example, spell-checking and file management 
- Having a conversation is good for certain services, for instance, finding information or requesting music
- Hybrid conceptual models are good for supporting multiple ways of carrying out the same actions

**Difference between interaction types and interface styles** 
*Interaction type*: 
- A description of what the user is doing when interacting with a system, for example, instructing, talking, browsing, or responding

*Interface style*:
- The kind of interface used to *support the interaction*, for instance, command, menu-based, gesture, or voice
- e.g. Command, Speech, Data-entry, Form fill-in, Query, Graphical, Web, Pen, Augmented reality


**Other sources** 
Conceptual knowledge that is used to inform design and guide research include:
- **Paradigms** - Inspiration for a conceptual model, General approach adopted by a community for carrying out research 
	- Shared assumptions, concepts, values, and practices 
	- For example, desktop, ubiquitous computing, Wearables, Pervasive computing, IoT ...
- **Visions** - a driving force that frames research and development
	- imagine what life will be like in 10, 15, or 20 years’ time
	- Provide concrete scenarios of how society can use the next generation of imagined tech.
	- Also raise ethical questions such as, privacy and trust 
- **Theories** - Explanation of a phenomenon, such as how the mind processes information
	* Help identify relevant factors for designing and evaluating interactive products, such as Cognitive, social, and affective aspects
	* can be used to predict how users will interact with different interfaces
- **Models** - A simplification of an HCI phenomenon, Enables designers to predict and evaluate alternative designs 
	- Abstracted from a theory coming from a contributing discipline
- **Frameworks** - Set of interrelated concepts and-or specific questions for ‘what to look for’
	- Provide advice on how to design user experiences
	- Focus on how to design particular kinds of interfaces to evoke certain responses
	- Such as steps, questions, concepts, challenges, principles, tactics, and dimensions 

**A classic HCI framework** 
*Don Norman's HCI Framework* - framework of the relationship between the design of a conceptual model and a user’s understanding of it
The framework consists of three interacting components:
1. **The Designer's Model**: The designer's understanding of how the system should work.
2. **System Image**: How the system actually works, as presented to the user through the interface and documentation.
3. **The User's Model**: The user's understanding of how the system works, which may or may not match the designer's model.

# CH 4: COGNITIVE ASPECTS
### Cognition
**Cognition** - Thinking, remembering, learning, daydreaming, decision-making, seeing, reading, talking, writing…
- Provides knowledge about what users can and cannot be expected to do
- Identifies and explains the nature and causes of problems that users encounter
- Provides theories, modeling tools, guidance, and methods that can lead to the design of better interactive products

##### Attention
**Attention** - Selecting things on which to concentrate at a point in time from the mass of stimuli around us
*Design recommendation* - Information at the interface should be structured to capture users’ attention, e.g., use boundaries (windows), color, reverse video, sound, and flashing lights 
*Multitasking* can cause people to lose their train of thought, make errors, and need to start over

**Design implications for attention** 
*Context*: Make information salient (stand out) when it needs to be attended to at a given stage of a task. use colors, ordering ..., avoid cluttering interfaces with too much info

##### Perception
**Perception** - How information is acquired from the world and transformed into experiences
*Obvious design implication* - design representations that are readily perceivable
- Text should be legible, contrast properly
- Icons should be easy to distinguish and read
- Bordering and spacing are effective visual ways of grouping information

##### Memory
**Memory** - recalling various kinds of knowledge that allow people to act appropriately
- We recognize things much better than being able to recall things
*Processing in memory* 
- Encoding is first stage of memory
- The more attention paid to something, the more processing, more likely to be remembered.
- *Context* affects the extent to which information can be subsequently retrieved - e.g. seeing your neighbor on the train and forgetting their name b/c you are used to seeing them on the hallway

*Recognition vs recall* 
- command based interfaces require recalling the command names
- GUIs provide visual options that only require recognition
	- browser history supports recognition

George Miller’s (1956) theory of how much information people can remember
- People’s immediate memory capacity is very limited to $7\pm 2$, 5 to 9 items
However, this doesn't strictly apply to interface design elements like menus, toolbars, or lists.
**The reason is:** users scan these elements visually rather than having to recall them from memory. Therefore, designers can present more than nine items. The number of items ultimately depends on the specific task and available screen space.

*Personal Information Management* (PIM) is challenging due to the sheer volume of digital assets. Users struggle to save, name, and retrieve files, with naming being a common but often ineffective encoding method.
Bergman and Whittaker's 2016 model proposes three processes to manage digital "stuff": deciding what to keep, organizing it for storage, and strategizing for retrieval. While folders and naming are prevalent, users prefer scanning. Search engines are only effective if the file name is known, though "smart" search engines can assist with partial names.

**Design implications** - Reduce cognitive load by avoiding long and complicated procedures for carrying out tasks
- Design interfaces that promote recognition rather than recall
- Provide users with various ways of labelling digital information - folders, categories, colors ...

##### Learning
*Learning* - accumulation of skills and knowledge involving memory.
**Learning** 2 types:
- *Incidental learning* (for example, recognizing people’s faces, what you did today)
- *Intentional learning* (for instance, studying for an exam, learning to cook) (is harder)
People prefer to learn by doing rather than instructions in a manual.

**Design implications** 
- design interfaces that encourage exploration
- that constrain and guide learners

##### Problem-solving, planning, reasoning, and decision-making
All these processes involve *reflective cognition*.
- Thinking about what to do, what the options are, and the consequences 
Often involves conscious processes, discussion with others (or oneself), and the use of artifacts.
May involve weighing up alternatives.

**Design implications** - Use simple and memorable functions to support rapid decision-making and planning.

### Cognitive frameworks
- used to explain and predict user behavior at the interface
	- Based on theories of behavior
	- Focus is on mental processes that take place
	- Also use of artifacts and representations

Most well frameworks known are:
- **Mental models** - Users develop an understanding of a system through learning about and using it
	- Internal constructions of some aspect of the external world enabling predictions to be made.
	- Involves unconscious and conscious processes.
	- Deep versus shallow models 
	- Most of us have *erroneous mental models* - Poor, often incomplete, easily confusable, based on inappropriate analogies and superstition.

- **Gulfs of execution** (gaps from the user to the physical system "what is the current system's state?") and **Gulf of evaluation** (gaps from the physical system to the user "how do i use this system?")
	- Bridging the gulfs can reduce cognitive effort required to perform tasks
	- *Information processing* - ->Encoding->Comparison->Response selection->Response execution
- **Distributed cognition** - Concerned with the nature of cognitive phenomena across individuals, artifacts, and internal and external representations.
	- Information is transformed through different media (computers, displays, paper, heads)
- **External cognition** - Concerned with explaining how we interact with external representations (such as maps, notes, and diagrams)
- **Embodied Cognition** - The practical engagement with the social and physical environment
	- Creating, manipulating and making meaning through our interaction with things
	- How our bodies and active experiences shape how we perceive, feel, and think

**Annotation and cognitive tracing** 
*Annotation* involves modifying existing representations through making marks.
- For example, crossing off, ticking, and underlining
*Cognitive tracing* involves externally manipulating items into different orders or structures.
- For instance, playing Scrabble or cards

**Embodied Interaction** - The practical engagement with the social and physical environment.
- Creating, manipulating and making meaning through our interaction with things
- They enable us to develop a sense of the world at both a concrete and abstract level

# CH 5: Social Interaction
**Conversational rules** 
Sacks et al. (1978) conversation analysis of conversations propose three basic rules:
- Rule 1: The current speaker chooses the next speaker by asking an opinion, question, or request
- Rule 2: Another person decides to start speaking
- Rule 3: The current speaker continues talking

Farewell rituals
- Bye then, see you, yeah bye, see you later….
Implicit and explicit cues
- For instance, looking at watch or fidgeting with coat and bags 
- Explicitly saying, “Oh dear, look at the time, I must go, I’m running late…”

**Remote conversations** 
VideoWindow system (Bellcore, 1989) - Shared space that allowed people 50 miles apart to carry on a conversation, 3 x 8 foot 'picture window' with video and audio.
**Findings:** 
- they talked about the system
- they talked more to the people in the same room ....

### Telepresence and social presence
**Telepresence** describes a situation where one individual feels present with another person who is physically located in a distinct, real-world space, such as a meeting room. In essence, it's about bridging geographical distance to create a sense of shared physical presence.

For example, imagine a video conference call where you're seeing and hearing colleagues who are all gathered in a conference room in a different city. If the technology is effective enough that you feel like you are *there* in the room with them, participating as if you were physically present, that's telepresence. The focus is on projecting one's presence into a remote physical location.

**Social Presence**, on the other hand, refers to the psychological sensation of being with a *real person* when you are immersed in a *virtual reality* environment. This concept specifically pertains to interactions within digital or simulated spaces.

For example, if you are wearing a VR headset and interacting with an avatar that is controlled by another human in real-time, and you have the feeling that you are genuinely interacting with a conscious, living person within that virtual world, that signifies social presence. It's about the feeling of authentic human connection and interaction within a simulated environment, rather than just with a computer program or a mere representation.

- **Telepresence:** Feeling like you are *physically present* in a *remote physical location* with others.
- **Social Presence:** Feeling like you are *with a real person* while you are in a *virtual environment*.

**What is co-presence?** 
Co-located groups who want to collaborate.
Many technologies have been designed to: 
- Enable groups to work, learn and socialize more effectively together
- For example, tabletops, whiteboards, and public displays

### Awareness mechanisms
Involves knowing who is around, what is happening, and who is talking with whom (**Dourish and Bly, 1992**)
**Peripheral awareness**: Keeping an eye on things happening in the periphery of vision
* Overhearing and overseeing allows tracking of what others are doing without explicit cues

**Situational awareness**: Being aware of what is happening around you to understand how information and your actions will affect ongoing and future events
* For example, air traffic control or an operating theatre

# CH 6: Emotional Interaction
Modern HCI - how to design interactive systems that make people respond in certain ways.
- happy, trusting, to learn, motivated ...

**Emotional interaction** is concerned with how we feel and react when interacting with technologies.

**How do emotions affect behavior and behavior emotions?** 
- Examine how people express themselves and read each other
	- For example, understand relationship between facial expressions, body language, gestures, and tone of voice. happy-relaxed, angry-screwed face

**Automatic (affect) Vs. conscious emotions** 
Emotions can be short-lived (a fit of anger) or complex and long-lasting (jealousy).
Two types of emotions:
- **Automatic** ones are rapid and dissipate quickly

# note from group
✅ 1. Fitts's Law (Paul Fitts, 1954)
Law: The time to acquire a target is a function of the distance to and size of the target.

Formula: T = a + b * log2(1 + D/W)

T: time to target, D: distance to target, W: width of target.

Cognitive/Affective Implication: Reduces user effort and frustration by making frequent or important buttons larger and closer.

Interface: Larger buttons for important actions (e.g., “Send” or “Buy”).

Persona: Users with motor impairments benefit greatly.

Conceptualization: Helps shape understanding of efficient movement and interaction.

✅ 2. Hick’s Law (William Edmund Hick & Ray Hyman, 1952)
Law: The time it takes to make a decision increases with the number and complexity of choices.

Formula: T = b * log2(n + 1)

Cognitive Implication: Reducing visible options speeds up decision-making.

Affective Implication: Minimizes choice overload and anxiety.

Interface: Use progressive disclosure; break tasks into steps.

Personas: Especially relevant for new users or those under stress.

✅ 3. Miller’s Law (George A. Miller, 1956)
Law: The average person can hold 7 ± 2 items in their working memory.

Cognitive Implication: Avoid overloading short-term memory.

Interface: Limit menu items, use chunking in lists.

Conceptualization: Promotes simplicity in mental models.

Personas: Crucial for novice users and those with cognitive disabilities.

✅ 4. Jakob’s Law (Jakob Nielsen)
Law: Users spend most of their time on other websites, so they prefer your site to work the same way.

Cognitive Implication: Leverages existing mental models to reduce cognitive load.

Affective Implication: Increases trust and comfort through familiarity.

Interface: Use common UI patterns and navigation styles.

Personas: All personas benefit, especially digital novices.

✅ 5. Nielsen’s Law of Internet Bandwidth (Jakob Nielsen, 1998)
Law: User connection speeds grow by 50% per year.

Implication: Designers can anticipate richer media in the future.

Interface: Enables use of higher resolution assets over time.

Personas: Design for the lowest common denominator but plan for future upgrades.

✅ 6. Tesler’s Law (Larry Tesler) – The Law of Conservation of Complexity
Law: Every application has an inherent amount of complexity. The user or the developer must handle it.

Cognitive Implication: Hide complexity from the user when possible.

Interface: Design for minimal steps and smart defaults.

Affective Implication: Reduces stress and frustration.

Conceptualization: Shift complexity to the backend.

✅ 7. Doherty Threshold (Walter J. Doherty, 1982)
Law: Productivity increases when system response time is under 400 ms.

Affective Implication: Fast systems feel more responsive, encouraging user engagement.

Interface: Prioritize performance optimization and feedback cues.

Personas: All users benefit from faster perceived response times.

✅ 8. Law of Prägnanz (Gestalt Psychology)
Law: People will perceive and interpret ambiguous or complex images as the simplest form(s) possible.

Cognitive Implication: Leverage users’ natural pattern recognition.

Interface: Use clean layout, grouping, hierarchy.

Conceptualization: Aids in visual mental model formation.

✅ 9. Postel’s Law (Jon Postel)
Law: Be liberal in what you accept, and conservative in what you send.

Cognitive Implication: Systems should be forgiving of user input errors.

Interface: Accept input in multiple formats (e.g., phone numbers with/without dashes).

Personas: Helps users with low digital literacy.

✅ 10. Aesthetic-Usability Effect
Law: Users perceive aesthetically pleasing design as easier to use.

Affective Implication: Enhances trust, perceived usability.

Interface: Invest in visual design—color, typography, spacing.

Personas: Especially influential for first impressions and consumer-facing interfaces.

**Usability**

**Principles of Usability:** 
1. **Learnability:** How easily new users can start using a system effectively and reach peak performance. principles of learnability:
	- **Predictability:** Users can anticipate future actions based on past interactions.
	- **Synthesizability:** Past actions clearly influence the current state (e.g., moving a file updates its location).
	- **Familiarity:** New users can readily grasp interaction styles and functions.
	- **Generalizability:** Domain-specific knowledge isn't required for common operations (e.g., cut/copy).
	- **Consistency:** The system's behavior and results are reliable, producing the same output for the same input.

2. **Flexibility:** The variety of ways users and the system can exchange information. principles of flexibility:
	- **Dialog Initiative:** Interactions are managed through a simple request and response system.
	- **Multi-threading:** A single codebase can be used concurrently by multiple processes.
	- **Task Migratability:** Tasks can be shifted between the system and the user based on who is better suited (e.g., a spell checker).
	- **Substitutivity:** Equivalent input and output values can be interchanged (e.g., percentages and grades).
	- **Customizability:** Users can modify the interface, with the system also supporting automatic adjustments.

3. **Robustness:** The degree of support provided to users for successfully achieving their goals. principles of Robustness:
	- **Observability:** Users can assess internal system features and receive appropriate feedback.
	- **Recoverability:** The system can correct errors and help users achieve correct actions.
	- **Responsiveness:** The system provides immediate feedback to user actions.
	- **Task Conformance:** The system fulfills all user requirements and interactions.

*Shneiderman’s 8 Golden Rules of Interface Design:* 
They aim to enhance usability and reduce errors, making systems more intuitive and efficient.
1. **Strive for consistency:** Maintain consistent sequences of actions, terminology, and visual elements.
2. **Enable frequent users to use shortcuts:** Provide accelerators like keyboard shortcuts or macros for experienced users.
3. **Offer informative feedback:** For every user action, the system should provide clear and immediate feedback.
4. **Design dialog to yield closure:** Organize sequences of actions into groups with a beginning, middle, and end, providing closure feedback.
5. **Offer simple error handling:** Design systems to prevent errors, and if they occur, offer simple, constructive, and specific instructions for recovery.
6. **Permit easy reversal of actions:** Allow users to undo actions easily, encouraging exploration without fear of irreversible mistakes.
7. **Support internal locus of control:** Make users feel in control of the system, not that the system is controlling them.
8. **Reduce short-term memory load:** Keep displays simple, consolidate information, and provide clear step-by-step guidance.

**Donald Norman's 7 Principles for Transforming Difficult Tasks:**
These principles focus on bridging the gap between human intentions and system operations, making complex tasks more manageable and user-friendly.
1. **Use Both Knowledge In the World and Knowledge in the Head:** Design should leverage both readily available external information (knowledge in the world, e.g., labels, signs) and the user's internal understanding and memory (knowledge in the head).
2. **Simplify the Structure of Tasks:** Break down complex tasks into smaller, more manageable steps to reduce cognitive load and make the overall process easier to understand and complete.
3. **Make Things Visible: Bridge the Gulf of Execution and Evaluation:** Ensure that all necessary information for executing an action and evaluating its outcome is readily visible. This helps users understand what they can do and what has happened.
4. **Get the Mappings Right:** Create clear and intuitive relationships between controls and their corresponding effects. The layout of controls should naturally indicate their function.
5. **Exploit the Power of Constraints, Both Natural and Artificial:** Use physical, semantic, cultural, and logical constraints to guide users and prevent errors by limiting possible actions to appropriate ones.
6. **Design for Error:** Anticipate potential user errors and design systems that minimize their occurrence, detect them when they happen, and provide easy ways for users to recover without frustration.
7. **When All Else Fails, Standardize:** For situations where intuitive design is difficult, establish and adhere to clear standards and conventions. This provides predictability and reduces the need for users to learn new interactions for common tasks.

### PART I: TRUE / FALSE

**1. Low-fidelity prototyping is inappropriate for iterative testing with end users in agile environments.**

**Answer: FALSE**

Low-fidelity prototypes (paper sketches, cardboard mockups) are actually *ideal* for early iterative testing in agile environments. They are fast to produce, cheap to modify, and encourage open feedback because users feel comfortable criticising something that looks unfinished. Agile development relies on rapid build-test-refine cycles; low-fi prototypes fit perfectly into sprints. High-fidelity prototypes are more appropriate *later*, when fine interaction and visual details need evaluation.

Key concepts: Low-fidelity prototype = rough approximation used to explore ideas early. High-fidelity prototype = near-finished, detailed version used for later testing. Iterative design = repeatedly design → test → refine.

---

**2. The concept of "perceived affordance" can conflict with "actual affordance" in complex digital interfaces.**

**Answer: TRUE**

An *affordance* is the property of an object that signals how it can be used (originally coined by James Gibson; adapted to design by Don Norman). Actual affordance is what the object truly allows (e.g., a button that can be clicked). Perceived affordance is what the user *thinks* they can do with it (e.g., a flat coloured rectangle that looks interactive but is actually a static label). In digital interfaces these often conflict: a visual element may look clickable (perceived affordance) but do nothing (no actual affordance), or vice versa — a deeply hidden interactive element has actual affordance but no perceived one. This is a classic usability problem.

From the textbook (Ch. 7): the affordances of large, colourful buttons make it easy to know what is being asked of a passerby (Happyornot terminals). If the visual design were removed, the perceived affordance would disappear even though the actual functionality remained.

---

**3. Responsive design only applies to adapting layout dimensions, not user behavior across contexts.**

**Answer: FALSE**

Responsive design encompasses far more than layout reflow for different screen sizes. It also involves adapting content priority, navigation patterns, input mechanisms (touch vs. mouse), and interaction flows to suit the context of use. On mobile, designers hide secondary content, enlarge touch targets (Fitts' Law), and simplify navigation — all behavioural adaptations. Context-aware responsive design adjusts to location, connectivity, and user state, not merely pixel dimensions.

---

**4. Designing for cross-cultural usability requires semantic deconstruction of visual metaphors.**

**Answer: TRUE**

Visual metaphors are culturally situated. The textbook (Ch. 3) explains that interface metaphors rely on users' *familiar* knowledge. A "mailbox" metaphor for memory, used in Western AI tools, may be meaningless to users from cultures where physical mailboxes are uncommon (see Case-8 on page 5 of the exam). Cross-cultural usability design requires analysing and potentially replacing or supplementing metaphors whose semantic meaning does not transfer across cultures. This is exactly what "semantic deconstruction" means — taking apart a metaphor and examining what cultural knowledge it assumes.

---

**5. A/B testing is a qualitative method for assessing emotional user engagement in digital products.**

**Answer: FALSE**

A/B testing (split testing) is a **quantitative** method. It compares two design versions by measuring user behaviour metrics such as click-through rates, conversion rates, and task completion times — producing statistical data about which version performs better. Assessing *emotional* engagement is typically done through qualitative methods (interviews, think-aloud protocols, diary studies) or specialised affective computing tools (facial expression analysis, physiological sensors).

---

**6. Designing a tangible user interface for smart retail environments requires less cognitive load consideration than designing for mobile GUI-based interfaces.**

**Answer: FALSE**

Tangible user interfaces (TUIs) do not automatically reduce cognitive load. While the textbook (Ch. 7) notes that TUIs exploit users' existing knowledge of physical object manipulation, smart retail environments introduce unique cognitive challenges: multiple simultaneous interactions, physical navigation through a space, interpreting sensor feedback, and recovering from physical errors. GUI-based mobile interfaces, while visually complex, are bounded to a single screen, providing constrained and predictable interaction. Cognitive load must be carefully managed in *both* interface types.

**7. Mega menus improve user navigation efficiency on mobile-first e-commerce platforms.**

**Answer: FALSE**

The textbook (Ch. 7) states that mega menus are "popular with online shopping sites, where lots of items can be viewed at a glance on the same screen without the need to scroll." However, this applies to *desktop* interfaces. On mobile-first platforms, mega menus are problematic because they require precision hovering, overflow small screens, and increase touch error rates. For mobile, flat or collapsible (hamburger) menus are preferred. Usability research confirms mega menus benefit desktop but are detrimental on mobile.

**8. Ethical UX design only applies to user privacy, not behavioral influence or addictive interaction loops.**

**Answer: FALSE**

The textbook (Ch. 6) devotes significant discussion to persuasive design, dark patterns, and ethical concerns. Ethical UX encompasses: user privacy yes — but also preventing manipulative persuasion techniques, avoiding addictive loop designs (variable reward mechanisms used in social media and games), protecting vulnerable users (especially children), preventing scamming, and designing for overall well-being. Don Norman's concept of emotional design and the field of *positive computing* both stress that designers have a responsibility to the holistic well-being of users, not merely their data privacy.

**9. A chatbot-based customer support system can replace the need for visual accessibility design.**

**Answer: FALSE**

A chatbot addresses one *modality* of interaction (text/speech conversation) but cannot replace visual accessibility design, which covers: colour contrast for colour-blind users, screen reader compatibility, keyboard navigation, font sizing, alt-text for images, and more. Users who are deaf or hard of hearing may rely entirely on visual interfaces. Visual accessibility and conversational accessibility are complementary, not substitutes.

**10. A pilot study helps evaluate the effectiveness of data analysis methods.**

**Answer: FALSE**

A pilot study is a small-scale preliminary study used to test and refine *research procedures* — checking that interview questions are clear, equipment works, and procedures run smoothly — *before* conducting the full study. Its primary purpose is to test *methodology*, not to evaluate the effectiveness of data analysis methods specifically. Data analysis methods are evaluated through validation studies, statistical tests, and comparison with ground truth data.

**Q2. Construct an ecosystem map of sensors, inputs, and interaction touch-points for a smart mobility interface adapting to driver stress. Discuss ethical implications.**

**Sensors / Inputs:**


* Biometric: heart rate variability (HRV) monitor embedded in the steering wheel; skin conductance sensor in the seat; eye-tracking camera monitoring blink rate and pupil dilation.
* Behavioural: driving speed, lane deviation frequency, braking force, steering micro-corrections.
* Environmental: GPS (traffic density, road type); microphone (voice stress analysis); ambient light sensor.
* Explicit user input: a manual mood check-in button; voice commands.

**Processing layer:**

An emotional state inference engine (ML model trained on multimodal sensor fusion) classifies stress level as Low / Moderate / High in real time.

**Interaction touch-points / outputs:**

* Haptic feedback pulsed through the steering wheel when stress is high.
* Ambient interior lighting shifts to warm, calming tones.
* Proactive audio nudge: "Your heart rate is elevated. Would you like to take a break?" — a *responding* interaction type (textbook Ch. 3).
* Navigation system automatically re-routes to less congested roads.
* Music environment adapts (calm audio selected automatically).
* Optional dashboard alert to a fleet manager for commercial drivers.

**Ethical implications:**

1. Consent and transparency — drivers must know exactly what biometric data is being collected, how it is processed, and who can access it. Continuous monitoring without explicit, ongoing consent violates autonomy.
2. Data privacy — biometric and location data are highly sensitive. Robust storage, access controls, and clearly defined retention policies are mandatory.
3. Over-reliance and deskilling — if the system perpetually manages driver stress, users' own emotional self-regulation skills may atrophy over time.
4. Discrimination risk — if stress data is shared with insurers or employers, it could enable discriminatory practices against drivers who exhibit higher stress profiles.
5. False positives — a system that incorrectly identifies stress and intervenes could introduce distraction and additional anxiety, worsening the situation it was designed to prevent.
6. Autonomy vs. paternalism — proactive interventions (changing music, re-routing without permission) may feel intrusive. Users must be able to override the system easily and immediately.

The textbook (Ch. 6, Norman 2005) notes: "things intended to be used under stressful situations require a lot more care, with much more attention to detail."

**Q3. How does vertical prototyping differ from horizontal prototyping? (1 point)**

A **horizontal prototype** demonstrates the *breadth* of a system — it covers all features and screens at a surface level, showing what the complete product will look like, but none of the features are fully functional or deeply implemented. It is useful for stakeholder presentations and early UX walkthroughs.

A **vertical prototype** demonstrates the *depth* of one specific feature or workflow — all layers of the system (interface, logic, data) are implemented for that single function, while the rest of the system is absent. It is used to test technical feasibility and detailed usability of one critical path.

Analogy: A horizontal prototype is a floor plan of an entire house where no room is built. A vertical prototype is one fully furnished, fully functioning room built to completion.

In Case-6 of this exam, the emergency glucose alert prototype is *vertical* — testing one critical safety function at high fidelity.

**Q3. Techniques for helping users locate a specific window when multiple windows are open (3 pts.)**

2. **Alt+Tab / Exposé / Mission Control** — Keyboard shortcut (Alt+Tab on Windows; Mission Control on Mac) that displays all open windows simultaneously as thumbnails, enabling fast visual scanning and single-click selection.

5. **Colour coding and application icons** — Distinctive application icons in the taskbar and on window title bars provide rapid recognition cues that do not require reading.

**Q4A. "Complete Purchase Now!" dark pattern — how does it exploit users and what harm could it cause? (2 pts.)**

The textbook (Ch. 6) directly discusses this phenomenon: Schaffer (2009) "pointed out how many websites are designed to persuade or influence rather than enable people to perform their tasks." The large, colourful "Complete Purchase Now!" button exploits several cognitive mechanisms:

*How it exploits:* The button uses *visual salience* to trigger the visceral response level (Norman's model, Ch. 6) — its size and colour draw the eye and prompt impulsive clicking before the user has consciously reviewed the final price, checked for hidden fees, or confirmed delivery details. The design makes one action (buy immediately) far more prominent than alternatives (continue browsing, return to cart, remove items), creating strong *action bias*. *Potential harms:* Users purchase items they did not intend to buy, or at prices they did not consciously agree to, causing direct financial harm. When users later realise they were manipulated, they lose trust in the retailer. At a societal level, the widespread use of such dark patterns normalises deceptive design and erodes user confidence in digital commerce.

**Q4B. Smart home privacy settings with complex checkboxes and nested menus — how does this discourage users, and what alternatives could improve the experience? (3 pts.)**

*How this design discourages users:*

The design uses *complexity as a barrier* — a deliberate or negligent dark pattern called *sludge* (Sunstein, 2019). By making privacy settings a "complex web of checkboxes and nested menus," the design exploits *status quo bias*: when changing settings is cognitively expensive, most users abandon the attempt and accept defaults — which are typically the least privacy-protective options. This violates Norman's principle of *error prevention* (from the seven stages of action) by not clearly communicating the consequences of each checkbox, leaving users unable to make informed decisions. The cognitive load (Ch. 4 of the textbook) of navigating nested menus far exceeds what is reasonable, effectively deterring users from exercising their legal and ethical rights.

*Alternatives that would improve the experience:*

1. **Privacy dashboard with clear categories and simple toggles** — A single visual screen showing settings grouped by category (Location, Microphone, Data Sharing) with ON/OFF toggles and a single sentence explaining each one: "This lets the app know where your home is. 2. **Plain-language explanations** — Replace technical or legal jargon with simple, direct language. Users should be able to understand every setting without a technical background.
3. **Privacy setup wizard** — A step-by-step guided onboarding flow that walks users through each privacy decision individually, with a clear binary choice at each step, rather than confronting them with the full complexity at once.
4. **Progressive disclosure** — Show the three or four most important privacy settings by default; hide advanced or rarely-needed options under an expandable "Advanced settings" section accessible to those who want it.

### 1\. THE DOUBLE DIAMOND MODELS (⚠️ Frequently Tested \& Confused)

#### Classic Double Diamond (Design Council, 2004/2005)

The shape of two adjacent diamonds represents alternating **divergent** (opening up, exploring) and **convergent** (narrowing down, deciding) thinking. The first diamond addresses the *problem*; the second addresses the *solution*.

|Phase|Type|What happens|
|-|-|-|
|**Discover**|Divergent|Research, observe users, gather insights — understand the problem, not assume it|
|**Define**|Convergent|Synthesise research into a clear problem statement; affinity maps, journey maps|

* ❌ "Discover = come up with solutions" → **No.** Discover is purely about understanding the problem.
* ❌ "The process is strictly linear" → **No.** Teams iterate and loop back.
* ❌ "Define = designing the UI" → **No.** Define = articulating the problem clearly.

### 3\. AFFORDANCES (⚠️ Frequently Tested)

Originally from James Gibson (ecological psychology); adapted to design by **Don Norman**.

|Term|Definition|
|-|-|
|**Affordance (Gibson)**|Any action possibility an environment offers an organism — objective, independent of perception|
|**Perceived affordance (Norman)**|What a user *thinks* they can do with an object based on its appearance|
|**Actual affordance**|What the object truly allows the user to do|
|**Affordance conflict**|When perceived and actual affordances contradict each other — user is misled|
|**Signifier**|A visual/physical cue that communicates how to use something (e.g. a "push" label on a door)|

**Examples:**

* A flat coloured rectangle that looks like a button but does nothing → perceived affordance without actual affordance
* A clickable link with no underline or colour → actual affordance with no perceived affordance
* A door with a handle that should be pushed → affordance conflict (misleading signifier)

> ⚠️ **Exam trap:** Affordance ≠ usability. Affordance is specifically about the *perceivable signal of possible action*. Invisible sensors on a wristband = affordance challenge (you can't see what they do).

---

### 4\. NORMAN'S SEVEN STAGES OF ACTION

Don Norman (1988) — describes how users move from goal to action and back.

1. **Forming the goal** — What do I want to achieve?
2. **Forming the intention** — How will I try to achieve it?
3. **Specifying an action** — What exactly will I do?
4. **Executing the action** — Doing it physically
5. **Perceiving the state of the world** — What happened?
6. **Interpreting the state of the world** — What does it mean?
7. **Evaluating the outcome** — Did I achieve my goal?

**Gulf of Execution** = gap between user's intention and available actions (is it clear *how* to do what I want?)

**Gulf of Evaluation** = gap between system state and user's perception (is it clear *what happened*?)

> ⚠️ **Exam trap:** "Optimization" is **NOT** one of the 7 stages. The stages are about cognition and action, not system performance.

### 9\. THE KEY LAWS (⚠️ Always examined)

| Law                                  | Formula / Principle                                                         | Application                                                 |
| ------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- |

|**Ecological Perception**|We perceive the world directly as action possibilities (affordances)|Gibson (1977)|

> ⚠️ **Frequently confused:** Distributed cognition = humans + tools + environment together. Embodied cognition = the *body itself* shapes thinking. Ecological perception = direct perception of affordances in the world.

**Exam question pattern:** "Which framework emphasises interaction between humans, technology, and environment?" = **Ecological Perception** or **Distributed Cognition** depending on emphasis. Ecological = direct perception/affordances; Distributed = cognitive work spread across a system of people and artifacts.

### 15\. COLOUR IN UI DESIGN

#### Colour Wheel

* A **circular arrangement of colour hues** showing relationships
* Complementary = opposite on wheel; Analogous = adjacent; Triadic = equidistant
* NOT a linear sequence, NOT just primary + secondary colours

* Most common: **red-green** (deuteranopia/protanopia) — affects \~8% of males

#### Colour Psychology for Motivation

* **Red** = energy, urgency, arousal → best for exercise/fitness apps
* **Blue** = calm, trust → banking, health
* **Green** = nature, calm → sustainability, health
* **Yellow** = caution, attention → warnings, highlights

### 16\. ETHICAL UX DESIGN (Ch. 6)

#### Dark Patterns

Designs that manipulate users against their interests:

* **Visual salience trick** — making "buy" button huge and colourful to trigger impulsive action
* **Pre-checked boxes** — items automatically added to cart; user must actively deselect
* **Sludge** — deliberately complex privacy settings to discourage users from exercising rights
* **Confirmshaming** — e.g., "No thanks, I don't want to save money"
* **Roach motel** — easy to get in (subscribe), hard to get out (cancel)

#### Privacy \& Consent Design

Good consent design:

* Plain language, no legal jargon
* Explicit ON/OFF toggles per data type
* "Ask me later" option
* Explains *why* data is collected, not just *what*

#### Ethical UX is NOT just about privacy — it also covers:

* Preventing addictive interaction loops
* Protecting vulnerable users (elderly, children)
* Avoiding discriminatory algorithmic outputs
* Designing for well-being, not just engagement

### 17\. PERSUASIVE DESIGN (Ch. 6)

* **Persuasive technology** (Fogg, 2009) = using technology to change behaviour
* Includes: nudges, reminders, rewards, social pressure, gamification
* Examples: fitness streak counters, energy consumption displays, donation prompts
* **Positive persuasion** (improving well-being) vs. **manipulative persuasion** (dark patterns) — ethical line is about user benefit vs. exploitation
* Textbook example: Nintendo Pokémon Pikachu pedometer — positive behavioural design

### 18\. INTERFACE METAPHORS (Ch. 3)

* Metaphors help users understand unfamiliar systems by mapping to familiar knowledge
* **Classic metaphors:** Desktop (Xerox Star 1981), Shopping cart, Search engine, Folder/File

### CHAPTER 1 — INTRODUCTION TO HCI

#### What is HCI?

"The scientific study of the interaction between people, computers, and the work environment."

Key definitions:

* **ACM (1992):** Discipline concerned with the design, evaluation and implementation of interactive computer systems for human use
* **Dix (1998):** Study of people, computer technology, and the ways these influence each other
* **Carroll (2002):** Study and practice of usability — creating software people will want to use, can use, and find effective

#### Three Components of HCI

* **Computer** — any device: desktop, embedded system, smartphone, photocopier
* **Human** — individual user, group, sequence of users in an organisation
* **Interaction** — a dialog with feedback and control throughout performing a task

#### Goals of HCI

* **Safety** — protect users from dangerous conditions and prevent catastrophic errors (e.g. nuclear plants, ICU equipment)
* **Utility** — provides the right kind of functionality for what users need
* **Effectiveness** — user's ability to accomplish a desired goal
* **Efficiency** — how quickly users can finish their work
* **Usability** — ease of learning and ease of use
* **Appeal** — how well the user likes the system (first impression + long-term satisfaction)

#### Benefits of HCI

Gaining market share, improving productivity, lowering support costs, reducing development cost.

#### Historical Roots of HCI

* **Computer graphics** — CRT and pen → mouse, bitmapped displays, desktop metaphor, point-and-click
* **Operating systems** — system response time tuning, windowing, multiprocessing
* **Human factors** — WWII aircraft display design, extended into cognitive HCI
* **Ergonomics** — study of work, posture, stress; → cognitive ergonomics
* **Industrial engineering** — efficient manual work methods, tool design, productivity
* **Cognitive psychology** — information processing, learning, mental representation of systems

#### Future of HCI

Ubiquitous communication, high functionality systems, mass graphics, mixed media, high-bandwidth interaction, large and thin displays, embedded computation, group interfaces, information utilities.

### CHAPTER 2 — THE HUMAN IN HCI

#### How Humans Process Information

Interaction with the world occurs through: **Senses (input) → Cognition (processing) → Effectors (output)**

Primary input senses: **Vision, Hearing, Touch** (most important in HCI)

Primary effectors (output): fingers, voice, eyes, head, body position

#### Vision — Two Stages

1. **Physical reception** — light → retina → rods (low light) → cones (colour) → electrical signals → brain
2. **Processing and interpretation** — brain interprets size, depth, brightness, colour

**Key visual concepts:**

* **Visual angle** — how much of view an object occupies; eye perceives size AND distance
* **Visual acuity** — ability to perceive fine detail; increases with luminance
* **Law of size constancy** — perception of depth and familiarity affect size judgement
* **Brightness** — subjective reaction to light levels; measured by just noticeable difference
* **Colour** — made of hue, intensity, saturation; cones detect colour; **8% males and 1% females are colour blind**
* **Constructivism** — brains do NOT create pixel-by-pixel images; minds construct models. Context plays a major role in what we see
* **Design implication of constructivism:** Do not expect people to "see" all interface details; context helps resolve ambiguity

#### Reading

* Eye makes **saccades** (jerky movements) followed by **fixations** (when perception occurs)
* Eye moves forwards and backwards = **regression** (increases with text complexity)
* Familiar words recognised by **word shape**

#### Hearing

* Sound characteristics: **Pitch** (frequency), **Loudness** (amplitude), **Timbre** (type/quality)
* Human hearing range: **20Hz to 15kHz**
* Less accurate distinguishing high frequencies than low

#### Touch (Haptic Perception)

* Receptors in skin: **Thermoreceptors** (heat/cold), **Nociceptors** (pain), **Mechanoreceptors** (pressure)
* Continuous pressure → receptors stop responding (adaptation)
* Fingertips are most sensitive/acute area
* Key sense for visually impaired users

#### Movement / Reaction Times

* Total response time = **reaction time + movement time**
* Reaction times by stimulus type:
* Visual: \~200ms
* Auditory: \~150ms
* Pain: \~700ms
* Increasing reaction time decreases accuracy in **unskilled** operators but NOT in skilled operators

#### Memory — Three Types

|Type|Access|Decay|Capacity|Notes|
|-|-|-|-|-|
|**Sensory memory**|Immediate|Very fast, continuously overwritten|Unlimited buffer|Iconic (visual), Echoic (aural), Haptic (tactile)|
|**Short-term (STM/Working)**|\~70ms|\~200ms|**5–9 chunks**|Scratch-pad for temporary recall; damaged by interference|
|**Long-term (LTM)**|\~1/10 second|Slow/none|Huge/unlimited|Episodic + Semantic|

**Passing information from sensory → STM:** Attention and filtering

**LTM Types:**

* **Episodic** — memory of events and experiences in serial form
* **Semantic** — structured record of facts, concepts, and skills derived from episodic LTM

**LTM Models (how semantic memory is structured):**

* **Semantic network** — nodes and links; inheritance (child inherits parent properties); supports inference
* **Frames** — data structures with slots and values; type-subtype relationships
* **Scripts** — stereotypical knowledge for interpreting situations (entry conditions, roles, scenes, tracks, results, props)
* **Production rules** — IF condition THEN action (procedural knowledge)

**LTM Activities:**

* **Storage** — rehearsal from STM stores in LTM; total time hypothesis; distribution of practice effect (spacing learning)
* **Forgetting** — Decay (information eventually lost) or Interference (retroactive: new info kills old; proactive inhibition: old info interferes with new)
* **Retrieval** — Recall (reproduced from memory, assisted by clues) vs Recognition (information gives knowledge it was seen before — simpler than recall)

> ⚠️ **Exam trap:** Recognition is less complex than recall — information itself acts as the clue.

#### Thinking

Types of reasoning:

* **Deductive** — derive logically necessary conclusion from premises. Note: logical conclusion is not necessarily true in the real world
* **Inductive** — generalise from cases seen to cases unseen (e.g. "all birds fly")

**Problem solving theories:**

* **Gestalt** — insight and restructuring of the problem
* **Analogy** — analogical mapping from similar domain (harder when domains are semantically different)
* **Problem space theory** — means-ends analysis; applied to well-defined areas like puzzles

**Skill acquisition — 3 levels:**

1. Learner uses general-purpose rules interpreting facts (slow, memory-demanding)
2. Develops task-specific rules using procedures
3. Rules tuned for speed — generalisation

#### Errors and Mental Models

* **Slip** — right intention, wrong execution (poor physical skill, inattention) → fix with better interface design
* **Mistake** — wrong intention, wrong understanding of system → fix with better user understanding of the system

#### Emotion

* **Affect** (biological response to physical stimuli) influences how we respond
* Positive affect → creative problem solving
* Negative affect → narrow thinking
* **Design implication:** Stress increases difficulty of problem solving; relaxed users are more forgiving; aesthetically pleasing interfaces increase positive affect

#### Individual Differences

Three causes:

* **Long-term** — gender, physical and intellectual abilities
* **Short-term** — stress or fatigue
* **Changing** — e.g. age-related changes

Design implication: **Blue acuity is poor** → do not use blue for important detail.

### CHAPTER 4 — INTERACTION

#### Key Terms

* **Domain** — area of expertise and real-world activity (e.g. graphic design, factory process control)
* **Goal** — desired output of a task (what you want to achieve)
* **Intention** — specific action required to meet the goal
* **Task** — the activity performed to achieve the goal

#### Norman's Seven Stages of Action (Execution/Evaluation Loop)

1. Establish the goal
2. Formulate intention
3. Specify actions at interface
4. Execute action
5. Perceive system state
6. Interpret system state
7. Evaluate system state with respect to goal

**Gulf of Execution** — mismatch between user's intended actions and actions allowed by the system (user can't do what they want)

**Gulf of Evaluation** — mismatch between system's presentation and user's expectations (user can't see what happened)

#### Abowd and Beale Interaction Framework

Extension of Norman — 4 parts with their own languages:

* **User** → articulation → **Input** → performance → **System** → presentation → **Output** → observation → **User**
* Interaction = translations between these languages
* Problems in interaction = problems in translation

Not restricted to computers; identifies ALL major components; allows comparative assessment.

#### Ergonomics (Human Factors in Interaction)

Study of physical characteristics of interaction: control design, environment, screen layout.

**Examples:**

* Controls grouped by function, frequency of use, or sequentially (safety-critical)
* Seating adaptable for all user sizes
* Health issues: posture, temperature, humidity, lighting, noise, time
* Colour use: red for warning, green for okay; awareness of colour blindness

**Industrial vs. Office interfaces:** 

| -              | Office  | Industrial                |
| -------------- | ------- | ------------------------- |
| Data type      | Textual | Numeric                   |
| Rate of change | Slow    | Fast                      |
| Environment    | Clean   | Dirty (oil-soaked mouse!) |

**Glass interfaces** (industrial computer screens replacing dials/knobs): cheaper, more flexible, multiple representations, precise values — but less familiar to industrial workers.

#### Interaction Styles

|Style|Description|Advantages|Disadvantages|
|-|-|-|-|
|**Command language**|Type text commands|Powerful, flexible, fast for experts|High learning curve, error-prone for novices|
|**Menus**|Select from displayed list|Easy to learn, reduces recall|Slow for experts, limited options|
|**Form fill / Query**|Fill in fields|Good for structured data entry|Limited interaction|
|**Direct manipulation**|Act on visible objects|Intuitive, immediate feedback, easy to learn|Resource-intensive, hard to automate|
|**WIMP**|Windows, Icons, Menus, Pointers|Standard desktop; familiar|Requires screen space, mouse skill|
|**Natural language**|English-like commands|Easy for novice|Ambiguous, hard to parse|

#### WIMP Elements

* **Windows** — screen areas for application display
* **Icons** — small graphic representations of objects or actions
* **Menus** — selection from list; flat, drop-down, pop-up, cascading
* **Pointers** — cursor showing position and action
* **Buttons** — initiates action; radio buttons (mutually exclusive), check boxes (non-exclusive)
* **Toolbars** — lines of icons; fast access to common actions; often customisable
* **Dialogue boxes** — pop-up windows requesting information or notifying of events
* **Palettes and tear-off menus** — detachable menu collections

#### Initiative

* **Old systems:** Computer had initiative (question-answer, batch)
* **WIMP systems:** User has initiative
* **Pre-emptive interface elements** — system wrests control back (e.g., modal dialogue boxes — can't proceed until completed)
* Modern principle: **minimise pre-emptive elements** to allow user maximum flexibility

#### Error and Repair

* Cannot always avoid errors — can make them easy to detect and repair
* Good dialogue design: reflect back understanding, seek confirmation, allow correction

#### Context

* Interaction affected by social and organisational context
* Presence of others: improves performance in familiar tasks, inhibits acquisition of new skills (fear of failure)
* Privacy important for learning and experimenting
* Motivation: fear, allegiance, ambition, self-satisfaction

#### Managing Value

Users adopt a system ONLY IF: **Perceived Value > Cost**

* Value: helps get work done, fun, good for others
* Cost: download time, money, learning effort

#### Paradigm Shifts in HCI

|Era|Paradigm|Characteristic|
|-|-|-|
|Early|**Batch processing**|Impersonal computing; punch cards|
|1960s|**Time-sharing**|Interactive computing|
|1970s|**Networking**|Community computing|
|1980s|**Graphical displays / WIMP**|Direct manipulation|
|1980s|**Microprocessor**|Personal computing|
|1990s|**World Wide Web**|Global information|
|2000s|**Ubiquitous computing**|Symbiosis of physical and electronic worlds|

### CHAPTER 5 — INTERACTION DESIGN IN THE SOFTWARE PROCESS

#### What is Interaction Design?

User-oriented field focusing on meaningful communication of media between people and technology. Covers: apps, websites, smart watches, sensors, GPS, social media, games, healthcare technologies.

#### Needs of Interaction Design

1. **Goals** — purpose, audience, expectations, reasons
2. **Constraints** — materials, standards, cost, time, safety, compatibility
3. **Trade-offs** — balance between goals and constraints based on user choices

#### Waterfall Model (Software Life Cycle Phases)

1. **Requirements specification** — capture what system must provide
2. **Architectural design** — high-level: system components and relationships
3. **Detailed design** — ER-model, DFD, sequence diagrams, use-case diagrams, flowcharts
4. **Coding and unit testing** — small programs developed and tested individually
5. **Integration and testing** — all units integrated; acceptance testing (ISO9241 requires "outside authority")
6. **Operations and maintenance** — patches, enhancements, feedback to all phases

**Advantages:** Simple, easy to manage, clear stages, good for small projects with clear requirements.

**Disadvantages:** High risk/uncertainty, no changing requirements, poor for complex/ongoing projects, difficult to measure progress within stages.

> ⚠️ **Exam trap:** Waterfall is sequential and cannot accommodate changing requirements — this is its biggest weakness for HCI work where user needs evolve.

#### Useful = Usability + Utility

Usability design occurs at ALL stages of the life cycle, not as a single isolated activity.

#### User Focus

* Start with the intended user(s)
* Know your users: Who are they? Qualifications, experience, age, computer literacy
* "Probably not like you" — develop as per user requirement, not developer need
* Methods: structured interviews, observation (watch what people do), imagination when users unavailable

**Persona** — imagined/surrogate user (not a real user); used to focus design

**Cultural Probes** — packages (maps, cameras, diaries) to gather inspirational data about people's lives, values, and thoughts

#### Scenarios

Stories for design — rich descriptions of user interaction with a system.

* Simplest design representation; one of the most flexible and powerful tools
* Force designers to think in detail and notice problems before they happen

**Why use scenarios?**

* Communicate with others (designers, clients, users)
* Validate other models (task models, dialog models, navigation models)
* Express dynamics (screenshots can't show behaviour)
* Reusability for other systems
* Generate test cases in implementation phase

**Types:**

* **Goal/task-based scenarios** — given goal, must be completed before deadline
* **Elaborated scenarios** — detailed description of user actions within goal
* **Evaluative scenarios** — used to assess whether design meets user needs
* **What-if scenarios** — explore alternative paths/edge cases

#### Navigation Design

* Users require a map of the system to navigate without getting lost
* Navigation structures: linear, hierarchical, web-like

#### Screen Design and Layout

Key principles:

* Organisation: group related items; consistent layout; alignment
* Optimise display (not maximise) — too much information causes overload
* Colour: use sparingly; maximum 5 colours; coding not decoration; cultural sensitivity

#### Prototyping

A tangible artifact or model, not a detailed description.

**Types by fidelity:**

* Low-fidelity (paper, cardboard) — fast, cheap, early exploration
* High-fidelity (interactive, detailed) — close to final product, later stages

**Rapid Prototyping** — develops prototype very quickly to save time and money; collects info on requirements and design adequacy.

**Offline rapid prototyping techniques:**

1. Paper and pencil — fastest; low cost; designer quick ideas
2. 3D mockups — shows all dimensions
3. Wizards (Wizard of Oz) — only front-end designed; simulates system response
4. Video prototyping — recorded/animated videos of user interaction

**Online rapid prototyping:**

1. Non-interactive simulation — graphical "sprites" (Adobe Illustrator, Photoshop)
2. Interactive simulation — HyperCard, WordPress, scripting languages; users interact directly

**Rapid prototyping advantages:** early evaluation, addresses users unable to state requirements, continued refinement, higher chance of acceptable system.

**Disadvantages:** Can be resource-heavy and costly; design phase extended; reluctance to discard prototype; unrealistic user expectations; long time before working system.

#### Wireframing

* A structural design of a website/interface at the layout level
* Used early in development to establish basic page structure before visual design

**Components of a wireframe:**

1. Title — purpose of application
2. How to get there — name and location
3. Concept — objectives for this panel
4. Contents/data — all content, buttons, icons
5. If/Then — user behavioural results
6. Navigation links — hyperlinks and navigation
7. Error conditions — errors from user behaviour

**Advantages:** involves client, finalises content/visual elements, efficient changes, saves time/cost, user-needs oriented.

**Disadvantages:** No design concepts or technical details; elements can shift when transferred.

#### Model-View-Controller (MVC)

Architectural pattern separating application into three components:

|Component|Responsibility|Used by|
|-|-|-|
|**Model**|Data-related logic; retrieves/manipulates/updates data|Programmer, developer, DB analyst|
|**View**|All UI logic; output representation (charts, tables, forms)|UI designer, end user|
|**Controller**|Interface between Model and View; handles business logic, requests, data manipulation|All three roles|

**Benefits:** Logical clustering; parallel development; model can have multiple views; scalable; useful for desktop, mobile, web.

### CHAPTER 6 — DESIGN RULES, GUIDELINES AND EVALUATION

#### Usability Defined

**ISO9241:** Effectiveness, efficiency, and satisfaction with which specified users achieve specified goals in a specified environment.

The three principles of usability:

#### 1\. Principles of Learnability

How easily new users begin effective interaction and achieve maximal performance:

|Principle|Meaning|
|-|-|
|**Predictability**|Effects of future actions can be determined from past interaction history|
|**Synthesizability**|Effects of past operations on current state are determinable|
|**Familiarity**|New users can leverage familiar knowledge of other apps|
|**Generalizability**|Specific knowledge extends to new situations in same domain (e.g. cut/copy applies everywhere)|
|**Consistency**|Same input always gives same result|

#### 2\. Principles of Flexibility

How the system supports multiple ways of user-system information exchange:

|Principle|Meaning|
|-|-|
|**Dialog initiative**|Simple request-response dialogs|
|**Multi-threading**|Single input/code used across multiple processors/stages|
|**Task migratability**|Execution of tasks can transfer between system and user (e.g. spell checker)|
|**Substitutivity**|Equivalent values can be substituted (e.g. percentage and grade)|
|**Customizability**|Interface can be modified by user or automatically|

#### 3\. Principles of Robustness

Level of support for user in achieving and assessing goals:

|Principle|Meaning|
|-|-|
|**Observability**|Users can evaluate internal system features; proper feedback given|
|**Recoverability**|System helps fix errors and get correct actions|
|**Responsiveness**|Real-time feedback on user actions|
|**Task conformance**|System supports all user requirements|

#### Design Standards

* Set by **BSI, ISO, or National Military Agencies**
* Hardware standards: stable, difficult to change, expensive
* Software standards: unstable, easy to change, flexible

#### Design Guidelines (7 Key Rules)

1. Maintain internal and external consistency
2. Be suggestive and general (not prescriptive)
3. Use familiar terms and user-friendly representations
4. Use guidelines that resolve design conflicts
5. Use direct manipulation whenever possible
6. Design should be aesthetically pleasing without compromising usability
7. Abstract guidelines (principles) for early lifecycle; detailed guidelines (style guides) for later lifecycle

#### Shneiderman's 8 Golden Rules (⚠️ Very frequently tested)

|#|Rule|Key idea|
|-|-|-|
|1|**Strive for consistency**|Same actions in similar situations; same terminology|
|2|**Cater to universal usability**|Novices to experts; disabilities; cultures; keyboard shortcuts for experts|
|3|**Offer informative feedback**|Keep user informed at every stage; meaningful and contextual|
|4|**Design dialogs to yield closure**|Tasks have beginning, middle, end; inform user of completion|
|5|**Prevent errors**|Design to avoid errors; clear error messages with hints to solve|
|6|**Permit easy reversal of actions**|Undo option; reduces anxiety; encourages exploration|
|7|**Support internal locus of control**|User is initiator, not responder; user feels in charge|
|8|**Reduce short-term memory load**|Keep displays simple; consolidate multi-page displays; sufficient training for codes|

#### Norman's 7 Design Principles

|#|Principle|
|-|-|
|1|Use both knowledge in the world and knowledge in the head|
|2|Simplify the structure of tasks|
|3|Make things visible: bridge gulfs of execution and evaluation|
|4|Get the mappings right (user intention maps clearly onto controls)|
|5|Exploit the power of constraints, both natural and artificial|
|6|Design for error (anticipate and build in recovery)|
|7|When all else fails, standardise|

#### Nielsen's 10 Heuristic Principles (⚠️ Very frequently tested)

|#|Heuristic|
|-|-|
|1|Visibility of system status|
|2|Match between system and real world|
|3|User control and freedom|
|4|Consistency and standards|
|5|Error prevention|
|6|Recognition rather than recall|
|7|Flexibility and efficiency of use|
|8|Aesthetic and minimalist design|
|9|Help, diagnosis and recovery from errors|
|10|Documentation and help|

#### UIMS — User Interface Management System

Separates application business logic from the GUI/presentation layer. Supports portability, reusability, multiple interfaces, and customisability.

**Architectures:**

1. MVC (Model-View-Controller)
2. Linguistic Model (Foley 1990)
3. **Seeheim Model** (Green 1985)
4. Smalltalk-based model
5. **PAC Model**

#### Seeheim Model (1985, Germany)
Three components:
* **Presentation** (GUI) — what input/output is available to the user; lexical parsing
* **Dialog Control** — communication between presentation and application; maintains state; modes
* **Application Interface Model** — manages communication with the application; semantic feedback

Layers: Lexical → Syntactic → Semantic

Drivers: **Portability and Modifiability**

#### PAC Model (Presentation-Abstraction-Control)
System as hierarchy of cooperating agents (triads); extends MVC for multiple agents.

Each agent has:

* **Presentation** — visible behaviour (visual/audio)
* **Abstraction** — data model and operations on data
* **Control** — flow of control; communication between presentation and abstraction; communication with other agents

**Advantages:** Multiple views on same data model; easy to plug in/replace views.

**Disadvantages:** Not suitable for agent-oriented apps (mobile/robotics); data model changes are expensive; view/controller division unclear.

#### Evaluation Techniques

Three goals of evaluation:

1. Assess extent and accessibility of system functionality
2. Assess users' experience of interaction
3. Identify specific problems with the system

**Evaluation through expert analysis:**

1. **Cognitive walkthrough** (Polson \& Lewis, 1990) — sequence of steps; expert simulates user interaction; can be done at any development stage
2. **Heuristic evaluation** (Nielsen \& Molich) — design phase; uses Nielsen's 10 heuristics; useful for evaluating early designs, prototypes
3. **Use of previous work** — existing evidence for design choices (menus, colours, icons)
4. **Model-based evaluation** — high-level models predicting user performance

**Evaluation through user participation:**

* **Laboratory studies** — controlled environment
* **Field studies** — natural environment
* **Observational techniques:** Think aloud, cooperative evaluation, protocol analysis, automated analysis, post-task walkthrough
* **Query techniques:** Interviews, questionnaires

**Choosing an evaluation method — 8 factors:**

1. Stage in development cycle
2. Style (lab vs. field)
3. Level of subjectivity/objectivity
4. Types of measures (qualitative vs. quantitative)
5. Information provided (low-level detail vs. high-level usability)
6. Immediacy of response needed
7. Level of interference with users
8. Resources required (time, money, equipment, expertise)

#### HCI Models

**1. Goal and Task Hierarchies Model**

* **Goals** — state user wants to achieve
* **Tasks** — activities to achieve goals
* **Actions** — physical interaction with device
* Techniques: GOMS (Goals, Operators, Methods, Selections), CCT (Cognitive Complexity Theory), HTA (Hierarchical Task Analysis)

**GOMS:**

* **Goals** — what user wants to achieve
* **Operators** — basic actions user performs
* **Methods** — decomposition of goals into sub-goals
* **Selections** — choosing between competing methods

**2. Linguistic Model**

Analyses user behaviour and cognitive difficulty through language between user and system.

* **BNF (Backus-Naur Form)** — describes syntax; terminals (concrete actions like click, move) and non-terminals (abstract expressions)
* **TAG (Task-Action Grammar)** — addresses structure plus commands; parameterised grammar rules; makes consistency explicit

**3. Physical and Device Model (human motor skills)**

* **Buxton's 3-state model:**
* State 0: pointer not in range/active (light pen off screen)
* State 1: mouse moving, no button pressed (hover)
* State 2: button pressed while moving (drag)
* **KLM (Keystroke Level Model)** — predicts time to complete tasks
* Physical operators: K (keystroke), P (pointing), H (homing — switch hand between mouse and keyboard), D (drawing)
* Mental operator: M (mental preparation)
* System operator: R (system response)

#### Hierarchical Task Analysis (HTA)

A structured breakdown of tasks into sub-tasks to understand user goals.

**Steps:**

1. Define task being analysed
2. Conduct data collection
3. Determine overall goal (top of hierarchy)
4. Determine sub-goals
5. Decompose sub-goals into operations (until you reach executable actions)
6. Develop plans (how goals are accomplished; sequences and alternatives)

**Advantages:** Compare design approaches; enable UX reuse; capture design patterns; critical path analysis; error analysis; evaluate against functional requirements; measure learnability.

**Disadvantages:** Not directly useful for determining design solutions; primarily descriptive; large tasks = time and labour intensive.

#### Dialog Design Notations

**Diagrammatic (easy to read at a glance):**

* **State Transition Networks (STN)** — sequence of user actions; check consistency; recognised current state
* **Petri nets** — oldest notation; places, transitions, arcs, tokens; simple dynamic behaviour model
* **State charts** — extend FSM; handle concurrency; active states, basic states, super states
* **Flowcharts** — sequential steps with decision branches
* **JSD (Jackson Structured Design)** — less expensive; greater clarity; used for task analysis

**Textual (easier for formal analysis):**

* **Grammars** — describe syntax of interaction language
* **Production rules** — terminals + non-terminals combined in sequence (+) or alternatives (|)

#### Computer Mediated Communication (CMC)

Communication between individuals via computer through internet/network.

|Type|Description|Examples|
|-|-|-|
|**Asynchronous**|Independent of real time|Email, web pages, video downloads|
|**Synchronous**|Real time between users|Messages, audio/video calls|

Applications: Email, message systems, video conferencing, virtual collaborative environments.

### ⭐ LAST-MINUTE HIGH-YIELD REVISION

**HCI Goals (SUEEA):** Safety, Utility, Effectiveness, Efficiency, (usability) Appeal

**Memory numbers:** STM = 5–9 chunks, \~70ms access, \~200ms decay. LTM = \~1/10s access, huge capacity.

**Reaction times:** Visual 200ms, Auditory 150ms, Pain 700ms.

**Colour blindness:** 8% males, 1% females. Blue acuity poor. Avoid green-red for colour-blind users.

**Reading:** saccades → fixations → regression. Word shape used for familiar word recognition.

**Errors:** Slip = right goal, wrong action (fix: better interface). Mistake = wrong goal (fix: better system understanding).

**Emotion and design:** Positive affect = creative/flexible. Negative affect = narrow/rigid. Stressed users less tolerant. Aesthetically pleasing interfaces increase positive affect.

**Abowd \& Beale:** User → (articulation) → Input → (performance) → System → (presentation) → Output → (observation) → User.

**Waterfall phases (6):** Requirements → Architecture → Detailed design → Coding/Unit testing → Integration/Testing → Operations/Maintenance.

**Shneiderman's 8 rules:** Consistency, Universal usability, Informative feedback, Closure, Error prevention, Easy reversal, Internal locus of control, Reduce STM load.

**Nielsen's 10 heuristics:** System status visibility, Real-world match, User control, Consistency, Error prevention, Recognition not recall, Flexibility, Minimalist design, Error recovery help, Documentation.

**Norman's 7 principles:** Knowledge in world+head, Simplify tasks, Make visible, Right mappings, Constraints, Design for error, Standardise.

**Seeheim Model layers:** Lexical (Presentation) → Syntactic (Dialog Control) → Semantic (Application Interface). Drivers: Portability and Modifiability.

**PAC:** Presentation (visible behaviour) + Abstraction (data model) + Control (connects the two + communicates with other agents).

**Waterfall's biggest weakness:** Cannot accommodate changing requirements.

**HTA disadvantage:** Primarily descriptive, not prescriptive — tells you what happens, not what to do about it.

**GOMS:** Goals, Operators, Methods, Selections.

**KLM operators:** K (keystroke), P (point), H (home hand), D (draw), M (mental), R (system response).

**Dialog design:** STN = state + transition. Petri nets = place + transition + arc + token. State charts = extend FSM with concurrency. JSD = less expensive, greater clarity.

**MVC vs PAC:** MVC = 3 parts, one agent. PAC = hierarchy of many cooperating triads (agents), developed FROM MVC for multi-agent systems.

**Paradigm order:** Batch → Time-sharing → Networking → Graphical/WIMP → Microprocessor/Personal → WWW → Ubiquitous computing.
