# CH 1: Introduction to Research Methods
**What is Research?** 
- (noun) Scholarly or scientific investigation or inquiry.
- (verb) To study (something) thoroughly so as to present in a detailed, accurate manner.

Original investigation undertaken in order to gain knowledge and understanding, including
- Work of direct relevance to the needs of commerce and industry and to the public and voluntary sectors
- Scholarship (research infrastructure)
- The invention and generation of ideas, images, performances and artifacts including design, where these.
- lead to new or substantially improved insights;
- The use of existing knowledge in experimental development to produce new or substantially improved materials, devices, products and processes, including design and construction.

Research by Sharp et al. (2002)
- Seeking through methodical process to add to one’s own body of knowledge and to that of others, by the discovery of non trivial facts and insights.

### Types of Research
**Basic vs Applied Research** 
1. *Basic (Fundamental or Theoretical)* Research is performed without thought of practical ends, producing general knowledge and an understanding of nature and its laws.
	- concerned with generalizations and with the formulation of a theory.
	- *descriptive* in nature exploring what, why and how questions.
2. *Applied (Practice-Oriented)* research deals with finding a solution or conclusion for an immediate problem in the industries or businesses by using well known scientific approaches, theories and/or principles.
	- to find solutions that may cure diseases, solve industrial problems, or develop technology.
	- prescriptive in nature, focusing on how questions.

**Descriptive vs Analytical** 
1. *Descriptive research* includes surveys and fact-finding enquiries of different kinds.
	- description of the state of affairs as it exists at present.
	- The main characteristic of this method is that the researcher has no control over the variables; s/he can only report what has happened or what is happening.
	- used for descriptive studies in which the researcher seeks to measure factors like frequency of shopping, brand preference of people, most popular media program etc.
2. *Analytical research* is using of facts already available then analyze these to make a critical evaluation.
	- Descriptive research attempts to determine, describe, or identify what is, while analytical research attempts to establish why it is that way or how it came to be.

**Evaluation Research vs Action Research** 
1. *Evaluation Research* studies the processes and outcomes aimed at attempted solution.
	- to improve human intervention within specific conditions, such as activities, time, and groups of people;
	- to judge the effectiveness of a program, policy, or product.
2. *Action Research* aims at solving specific problems within a program, organization, or community.
	- design and data collection in action research tend to be more informal, and the people in the situation are directly involved in gathering information and studying themselves.

**Conceptual vs. Experimental** 
- *Conceptual research* is used by philosophers to develop new concepts or to reinterpret existing ones.
- *Empirical research* is data based research which can be verified by observation or experiment.

### What we can do with research?
1. **Categorize** - forming a typology of objects, events or concepts, This can be useful in explaining which ‘things’ belong together and how. See patterns more clearly.
2. **Describe** - relies on observation as a means of collecting data. It attempts to examine situations  in order to establish what is the norm, i.e. what can be predicted to happen again under the same circumstances. *Questions*: `What is happening?` 
	- Example: A researcher observes classroom behavior over several weeks.
		- They record: How often students participate, When attention decreases, How long discussions last
		- they may conclude: “In most classes of this size, student participation decreases after 40 minutes.”
3. **Explain** - designed to deal with complex issues. Moves beyond ‘just getting the facts’ in order to make sense of the myriad other elements involved, such as human, political, social, cultural and contextual. *Questions*: `Why is it happening?` 
	- Example: Descriptive finding: “Student participation decreases after 40 minutes.”
	- Explanatory research asks:
		- Is it cognitive fatigue? Is the teaching style too passive? Are cultural norms discouraging speaking up? Is the classroom environment affecting engagement?
	- moving from observation to understanding causes.
4. **Evaluate** - This involves making judgments about the quality of objects or events.
	- Quality can be measured either in an absolute sense or on a comparative basis.
	- Example: Evaluating two database systems for a web application:
		- *Absolute* sense: Does MySQL meet the project’s scalability requirements?
		- *Comparative* sense: Is PostgreSQL faster than MySQL for complex queries?
5. **Compare** - Two or more contrasting cases can be examined to highlight differences and similarities between them, leading to a better understanding of phenomena.
	- Example: Compare Merge Sort and Quick Sort on large datasets.
	- Method: Implement both algorithms in code and test execution time, mem usage ...
	- Shows trade offs and helps to choose the right algorithm
6. **Correlate** - The relationships between two phenomena are investigated to see *whether and how* they influence each other.
	- The relationship might be just a loose link at one extreme or a direct link when one phenomenon causes another.
	- Example: Correlate network traffic load with packet loss rate to determine how congestion affects performance.
7. **Predict** - This can sometimes be done in research areas where correlations are already known.
	- Example: Predict CPU scheduling delays under heavy workloads using historical process execution data.
8. **Control** - Once you understand an event or situation, you may be able to find ways to control it. 
	- For this you need to know what the cause and effect relationships are and that you are capable of exerting control over the vital ingredients.
	- e.g. Control project risks by adjusting resource allocation based on system simulation models.
	- Control allows teams to manage cause-and-effect relationships, reducing risks and improving project outcomes.

### Qualities of a Good Researcher
1. **Analytical**: The ability to identify various aspects or components of a research problem as well as the ability to narrow a research work down to one or more of the identified components.
2. **Literature**: Ability to source, organize and critically appraise published work in the area of interest.
3. **Contextualization**: to relate a problem to his/her experience and/or immediate environment. It encompasses the ability to produce and describe cases or data from primary observations or relate historical data to new problems in the local context.
4. **Problem formulation**: Ability to formulate a viable research problem that is concise with adequate theoretical support and feasible solution strategies.
5. **Synthesis**: Ability to consider possible (alternative) solutions and formulate a solution that effectively address the problem either by integrating two or more solution or by coming up with a new or unique solution altogether.
6. **Communication**: Ability to communicate the research activity in a concise, transparent and coordinated manner. 
7. **Research ethics**: The guiding principles for carrying out research including how to use of other people’s ideas, data collection and research communication to prevent issues of research misconduct such as fraud and plagiarism.

### The Nature of Data
1. **Ephemeral**: True at a specific time and place. Can change (e.g., daily opinions) may quickly lose relevance. *e.g.* Server load changes every minute

Because data are not perfect, researchers say:
- “It seems that…”
- “It is likely that…”
- “The evidence suggests…”
Because:

### Data collection methods/ Steps in Research Process
1. **Selection of Research Problem** - topic should be practical, relatively important, feasible, ethically and politically acceptable .
2. **Literature Review** - Academic journals, conference and govt. reports and library mostly connected with the topics must be studied. 
3. **Making Hypothesis** - to draw the positive & negative cause and effect aspects of a problem.
	- Hypothesis narrows down the area of a research and keep a researcher on the right path.
4. **Preparing the Research Design** - the conceptual structure of the problem.
	- Daring R. “Design the information about sources, skill, time and finance is taken into consideration”
5. **Sampling** - It is a plan for taking its respondents from a specific areas or universe.
	- The sample may be of two types:
		1. Probability Sampling
		2. Non-probability Sampling 
6. **Data collection** 
	- Primary Data Collection:
		1. Experiment
		2. Questionnaire
		3. Observation
		4. Interview
	- Secondary data collection: 
		1. Review of literature
		2. Official and non-official reports
		3. Library approach
7. **Data Analysis**: Two main categories:-
	- *Data Processing*: it is sub-divided into the Data editing, Data coding, Data classification, Data tabulation, Data presentation, Data measurement
	- *Data Exposition*: is the process of communicating the findings of the analysis. 
		- has the following sub-categories Description, Explanation, Narration, Conclusion/Findings, Recommendations/Suggestions
8. **Hypothesis Testing** 
	- Research data is then forwarded to test the hypothesis. Are the hypothesis related to the facts or not? Result in accepting or rejecting the hypothesis.
9. **Generalization and Interpretation** 
	* **Generalization:** If a hypothesis is proven, the researcher can develop a broader theory or general principle based on the findings.
	* **Interpretation:** In research without a formal hypothesis, the researcher explains the findings based on existing theoretical frameworks to provide context and meaning.
10. **Report Preparation** 
The final report must be structured logically to communicate findings effectively.
Stage 1: Preliminary Matter
* **Title & Introduction:** Define the problem and provide background.
* **Navigation:** Include a Table of Contents, acknowledgments, and a list of charts or graphs.

Stage 2: Main Body
* **Core Content:** Detail the objectives, methodology, and hypothesis.
* **Structure:** Organize the report into clear chapters, each ending with a summary of findings.
* **Conclusion:** Synthesize the data to highlight the study's primary theme and outcomes.

Stage 3: Closing
* **Supporting Data:** Attach bibliographies, references, and appendices.
* **Visual Aids:** Include an index and any necessary maps or detailed illustrations for maximum clarity.

### Sampling methods
A process used in statistical analysis in which a predetermined number of observations are taken from a larger population.
If anything goes wrong with your sample then it will be directly reflected in the final result.
- **Sample** is the subset of the population. 
- The process of selecting a sample is known as **sampling**. 
- Number of elements in the sample is the **sample size**

There are lot of sampling techniques which are grouped into two categories as
- Probability Sampling
- Non- Probability Sampling

##### Probability (random) Sampling
Uses randomization to make sure that every element of the population gets an equal chance to be part of the selected sample.
**Simple Random Sampling** Every element has an equal chance of getting selected to be the part sample.
- used when we don’t have any kind of prior information about the target population.
**Stratified Sampling** divides a population into homogeneous subgroups (strata) and then randomly selects elements from each to ensure all groups are represented.
**Cluster Sampling** divides a population into groups (clusters) by traits like age or location. We randomly select entire clusters and include every member within them.
- *Single-Stage*: Randomly choose clusters and study everyone inside.
- *Two-Stage*: Randomly select clusters, then randomly sample individuals from within those selected clusters.
- *Multi-Stage*: A more complex version involving several rounds of random sampling across different levels (e.g., states → cities → neighborhoods).

##### Non Probability Sampling
More reliant on the researcher’s ability to select elements for a sample.
**Convenience Sampling** selected based on the availability. used when the availability of 
sample is rare and also costly. e.g. Researchers prefer this during the initial stages of survey research, as it’s quick and easy to deliver results.
**Purposive Sampling** based on the intention or the purpose of study. those who satisfy a certain requirement.
**Quota Sampling** we select a sample that mirrors the specific proportions of a population.
- Fixed Standards: You set "quotas" based on traits like age or gender (e.g., if a population is 45% female, your sample must be 45% female).
- Selection: You stop collecting data for a category once that specific quota is filled.
**Referral or Snowball Sampling** used in the situations where the population is completely unknown and rare. referral technique goes on, increasing the size of population like a snowball.

### Measurement of Data
The process of assigning numbers, symbols, or categories to variables according to defined rules so that they can be analyzed and interpreted.
*Nominal Scale* classifies data into categories or groups without any order or ranking.
- Only counting frequencies is possible
- Mutually exclusive categories
- e.g. business types, gender, religion, programming language

*Ordinal Scale* categories with a meaningful order or ranking, but differences between ranks are not equal.
- no arithmetic operation
- Relative position known, Exact difference unknown
- e.g. customer satisfaction (Poor, Fair, Good, Excellent), performance, education level...

*Scale (Interval Scale)* Interval and Ratio scales, which are numerical and allow statistical analysis.
- Ordered data, Equal intervals, No true zero
- e.g. temperature, IQ score, test score, software release years

*Ordered data, Equal intervals*, Has a true zero and Allows all mathematical operations
- e.g. revenue, age, income, LOC...

##### Interpretation of Data
Refers to the process of critiquing and determining the significance of important information, such as survey results, experimental findings, observations or narrative reports.
The varying scales include:
- **Nominal Scale**: non-numeric categories that cannot be ranked or compared quantitatively. Variables are exclusive and exhaustive.
- **Ordinal Scale**: exclusive categories that are exclusive and exhaustive but with a logical order. (i.e., good, very good, fair, etc., OR agree, strongly agree, disagree, etc.).
- **Interval**: a measurement scale where data is grouped into categories with orderly and equal distances between the categories. There is always an arbitrary zero point.
- **Ratio**: contains features of all three.

**Qualitative Data Interpretation** 
Data is not described through numerical values or patterns, but through the use of descriptive context (i.e., text).
techniques include:
- *Observations*: detailing behavioral patterns that occur within an observation group.
- *Documents*: resources can be coded and divided based on the type of material they contain.
- *Interviews*: best collection methods for narrative data. Enquiry responses can be grouped by theme, topic or category.

**Quantitative Data Interpretation** 
it involves the use of statistical modeling such as standard deviation, mean and median.
- *Mean*: numerical average for a set of responses.
- *Standard deviation*: reveals the distribution of the responses around the mean. It describes the degree of consistency within the responses;
- *Frequency distribution*: When using a survey, for example, frequency distribution has the capability of determining the number of times a specific ordinal scale response appears (i.e., agree, strongly agree, disagree, etc.). 
	- Frequency distribution is extremely keen in determining the degree of consensus among data points.


# CH 3: Data Analysis Methods 
**Analysis** - turning raw data into useful information to provide answer to questions being asked.

**Interpretation** is using the analysis to further explore your findings and understand the implications for your program. 
- In many cases, this means using additional information, such as vital statistics, population-based surveys, and qualitative data, to supplement the routine service statistics.

### Qualitative data analysis
The process of analyzing non-numerical and descriptive information.
- Supports further understanding of the “why” and “how” of issues/problems

Is an inquiry approach useful for exploring and understanding a central phenomenon.
- the inquirer asks participants broad, general questions, collects the views of participants in the form of words or images, and analyzes the information for description and themes.  

From this data, the researcher interprets the meaning of the information drawing on personal reflections and past research.
The structure of the final report is flexible, and it displays the researcher’s *biases and thoughts*.

##### Presentation of Qualitative Variables
The simplest way of presenting/summarizing a qualitative variable is by using a **frequency table**, which shows the frequency of occurrence of each of the different categories.
- Such a table could also include the *relative frequency*, which indicates the proportion or percentage of occurrence of each of the categories.
- The frequency table could then be pictorially represented by a *bar graph* or a *pie diagram*.

### Quantitative Data Analysis
The process of analyzing numerical information to better understand relationships between variables.
- Focuses on statistical analysis to better assess trends and patterns.
- Useful to better explain the “what” of issues.

Applies to both:
- *Categorical* (qualitative) data – ex. gender, occupation, blood type
- *Numerical* (quantitative) data – ex. age, blood pressure, heart rate

Most analyses are performed using statistical software (ex. R, STATA, SAS, and Python).
- When the quantitative variable is discrete (such as counts), a *frequency table* and a *bar graph* could also be used for summarizing it.
- Only difference is that the values of the variables *could not be reshuffled* in the graph, in contrast to when the variable is categorical or qualitative.

##### Types of Statistical Analysis
There are two main types of statistical analysis:
1. **Descriptive Statistics** - summarizes and describes the *main features* of a dataset.
* Four primary ways:
	+ **Central Tendency**: Describes the "center" of data
		- **Mean**
			- Population mean - sum of all the population values divided by the total  number of population values.
				- $\mu=\frac{\sum X}{N}$ 
			- Sample Mean - sum of all the sample values divided by the total number of sample values. 
				- $\bar{X}=\frac{\sum X}{n}$ 
		- **Median** - The midpoint of the values after they have been ordered in ascending or descending order.
			- *Properties of the Median* 
				* There is a unique median for each data set.
				* It is not affected by extremely large or small values.
				* It can be computed for ratio-level, interval-level, and ordinal-level data.
				* It can be computed for an open-ended frequency distribution if the median does not lie in an open-ended class (dataset where the last class has no clear upper limit.) 
		- **Mode** - The value of the observation that appears most frequently.
	+ **Dispersion**: Spread of values in the dataset
		- **Range** - $\text{R}=\text{Largest value} - \text{smallest value}$ 
		- **Mean deviation** - $MD = \frac {\sum | X - \bar X |}{n}$ 
			- X - the value of each observation
			- $\bar X$ - arithmetic mean
		- **Variance** -
			- Population variance $\sigma^2=\frac{\sum(X-\mu)^2}{N}$ - $\mu$ - arithmetic mean
			- Sample Variance = $s^2=\frac{\sum(X-\bar X)^2}{n-1}$ 
		- **Standard deviation** - most widely used measure of dispersion.  
			- Population SD $\sigma=\sqrt{\frac{\sum(X-\mu)^2}{N}}$ 
			- Sample SD, $s=\sqrt{\frac{\sum(X-\bar X)^2}{n-1}}$ 
		- The variance and standard deviations are nonnegative and are zero only if all observations are the same. 
		- The variance overcomes the weakness of the range by using all the values in the population.
	+ **Location**: Indicates where a specific data point falls in the distribution
		+ we first order the data in lowest to largest value.
		+ let $L_p$ refer to the location of a desired percentile. 
			+ $L_p = (n-1) \frac {P}{100}$ 
		- **Quartile** - $L_{25}$-1st quartile, $L_{50}$, $L_{75}$ .
		- **Decile** -  $L_{10}$-1st decile, $L_{20}$ ...
		- **Percentile** - $L_1$, .. $L_{99}$ 
	+ **Proportion and Rates**: Summarizes how often something happens
		- Proportion
		- Rate
		- Prevalence
		- Incidence
		- Odds ratio (OR)
		- Relative risk (RR)

Example: In a group of 100 patients, the average age is 60 years old and 70% of participants are women.

2. **Inferential Statistics** 
* Allows for making predictions or generalizations about a population based on a given sample
Example: In a group of 100 patients, a researcher wants to know if exercising daily lowers blood pressure. Inferential statistics will help determine if the result is applicable to a general population.

##### Grouping Data

* **Equal-width** (if possible): Groups are the same width if possible.

##### The Arithmetic Mean and Standard Deviation of Grouped Data
**Calculating the Arithmetic Mean of Grouped Data** 
$$[ \bar{x} = \frac{\sum f_i \bar{x}_i}{\sum f_i} ]$$ Where:
* $( \bar{x} )$ is the arithmetic mean of the dataset
* $( f_i )$ is the frequency of each group (i.e., the number of observations in each group)
* $( \bar{x}_i )$ is the mean/midpoint of each group
* $( \sum f_i )$ is the sum of all frequencies
* $( \sum f_i \bar{x}_i )$ is the sum of the product of each frequency and its corresponding group mean

**Calculating the Standard Deviation of Grouped Data**
$$[ s = \sqrt{\frac{\sum f_i (\bar{x}_i - \bar{x})^2}{\sum f_i}} ]$$
Where:
* $( f_i )$ is the frequency of each group, ${\sum f_i}$  would be n-1 if for a sample SD.
* $( \bar{x}_i )$/$(M)$ is the mean/midpoint of each group
* $( \bar{x} )$ is the arithmetic mean of the dataset
* $( \bar{x}_i - \bar{x} )$ is the difference between each group mean and the overall mean

# CH 4: Research Theory and Design
### Research Theory 
A theory is a conceptualization, or description, of a phenomenon that attempts to integrate all that we know about the phenomenon into a concise statement or question 
- in the research context, they typically function as a rich source of hypotheses that can be examined empirically. 

Research design is the conceptual structure in which research is conducted, and it constitutes the blueprint for collecting, measuring, and analyzing data (`Selltiz` et al, 1962).

Key decisions included in research design are:
* The nature of the study
* The purpose of the study
* The location of the study
* The nature of data required
* The methods of data analysis
* The techniques of data collection and report preparation ...

*What is Research Design?* - A framework or blueprint for conducting a Major research project.
Decisions regarding what, where, when, how much, by what means concerning an inquiry
or a research study constitute a research design. 

*Meaning of research design* A research design is the arrangement of conditions for collection and analysis of data in a manner that aims to combine relevance to the research purpose with economy in procedure.

Research design has the following parts:
* **Sampling design**: deals with selecting items for observation
* **Observational design**: relates to the conditions under which observations are made
* **Statistical design**: concerns how data is analyzed
* **Operational design**: deals with techniques for satisfying sampling procedures

**Features of a good research design** 
A research design appropriate for a particular research problem, usually has these features:
* The mean of obtaining information.
* Availability and skills of the researcher and staff
* **Objective** of the problem to be studied
* **Nature** of the problem to be studied
* Availability of time and money for the research work.

**Components of Research Design** 
* Design the exploratory, descriptive, and/or causal phases
* Define required information
* Specify measurement and scaling procedures
* Construct and pretest a questionnaire
* Specify sampling process and sample size
* Develop a plan of data analysis

#### Types of Research Design 
There are different types of research designs. They may be broadly categorized as:
1. Exploratory Research Design;
2. Descriptive and Diagnostic Research Design; and
3. Hypothesis-Testing Research Design 

##### Exploratory Research Design
AKA *formulative research design*.
Main objective - to formulate a research problem for an in-depth investigation, or for developing a working hypothesis.

The major purpose of such studies is the *discovery of ideas and insights*.

Should be flexible enough to provide opportunity for considering different dimensions of the problem under study.

The in-built flexibility in research design is required as the initial research problem would be transformed into a more precise one in the exploratory study, which in turn may necessitate changes in the research procedure for collecting relevant data.

Usually, the following three methods are considered in the context of a research design for such studies:
*   A survey of related literature
*   An experience survey
*   Analysis of ‘insight-stimulating’ instances

##### Descriptive and Diagnostic Research Design
**A Descriptive Research Design** is concerned with describing the characteristics of a particular individual or group.
- Specific predictions or narration of facts and characteristics related to an individual, group, or situation. 
- Most social research designs fall under this category.
**A Diagnostic research design** determines the frequency with which a variable occurs or its relationship with another variable. (Association)

Both descriptive and diagnostic studies share common requirements and are grouped together. 
However, the procedure to be used and research design need to be planned carefully.

The research design should:
* Formulate the objectives of the study
* Properly design the methods of data collection
* Select the sample
* Collect data
* Process and analyze the collected data
* Report the findings

##### Hypothesis-Testing Research Design 
Are those in which the researcher tests the hypothesis of causal relationship between two or more variables.
These studies require procedures that would not only *decrease bias* and *enhance reliability*, but
also facilitate *deriving inferences about the causality*. 
Generally, experiments satisfy such requirements. Hence, when research design is discussed in such studies, it often refers to **the design of experiments**.

### Classifications of Research Design Strategies in Software Engineering 
Research design strategies in software engineering may be classified into the following categories:
- Empirical
- Observational
- Correlational
- Quasi Experimental 

#### Empirical research methods
##### Controlled Experiments
* High level of control and repeatability
* AKA *research in-the-small*. - usually done in controlled environments to test isolated activities.
* "**True experiment**" an experiment conducted where an effort is made to impose control over all variables except the one under study.
* Researchers control independent variable(s) and observe dependent variable(s).
##### Survey Method Research
* Participants answer questions through interviews or questionnaires, then after, researchers describe responses given by participants.
* AKA *research in-the-large*. researchers observe a phenomenon in a representative subset of some population.
* Questions must be properly constructed for reliability and validity
* Types of questions include:
	+ Open-ended: allow for variety of responses but difficult to analyze statistically.
	+ Closed-ended: easy to analyze but limit responses
	+ Partially open-ended or Likert-type scale: easy to analyze and flexible response options
##### Case Study Research
* Involves an in-depth study of an individual or group of individuals
* Can lead to testable hypotheses and allow study of rare phenomena
- AKA "*research in-the-typical*"
* Has *limited use* for determining *cause and effect* or making accurate *predictions*.
* Needs careful documentation and multiple case studies can strengthen results

* Two serious problems:
	+ *Expectancy effects*: experimenters' biases can affect research
	+ *Atypical individuals*: may lead to poor generalizations and low external validity

* *Methods for data collection*: interviews, project participation, documents, and artifacts.
In case study, researchers observe phenomena in a real-life context i.e. collecting data about an ongoing project. 

#### Observational research methods
Observational method - phenomena are being observed and recorded and a detailed report with analysis would be written and reported constituting the study of this individual case. 
##### Field Observation
* There are two main categories of observational methods: 
	* **Quantitative** (based on measurement) and 
	* **Qualitative** (not based on measurement)
* Quantitative methods are suitable for sciences with an *empirical* nature, while qualitative methods are suitable for sciences with a *social and cultural* nature.

* Observational methods can be categorized into 
	* **Naturalistic Observation** (observing participants in their natural environment) and 
	* **Laboratory Observation** (controlling variables in a laboratory setting)
		* some argue lab observations are more meaningful due to more control
		* are usually less time consuming and cheaper

#### Correlational Research Methods
- Correlational research examines the *co-variation* of two or more variables.
- is a type of *observational research*, where nothing is manipulated by the experimenter or individual conducting the research.
- It is *not causal research*, meaning we cannot make statements about cause and effect.
- Correlational research is often conducted as *exploratory* or beginning research, to identify and define variables *for further experimentation*.

#### Quasi Experimental research methods
**Key characteristics**: 
* Very similar to true experiments but use naturally formed or preexisting groups.
* In naturally formed groups, the variable under study is a *subject variable* as opposed to an independent variable in true experiments, *limiting conclusions* we can draw from the research.
	* There are many differences between the groups that we cannot control and those could account for differences in our dependent measures.
* Cannot make statements of causality due to lack of random assignment.
* When a study uses pre-existing groups that are not naturally formed, the variable that is manipulated between the two groups is an *independent variable*.

**Design limitations**: 
* Can result from studying naturally formed groups or pre-existing groups
* Pre-existing groups can have differences other than the variable of interest

### Choosing the right research design
**Software engineering research answers questions** 
a. About method or means of development
- how can we do/create (or automate doing) X?  - what is a better way to do/create X?
b. About method for analysis
- how can I evaluate the quality/correctness of X?  - how do I choose between X and Y?
c. About details of design, evaluation, or analysis of a particular instance
- what is a (better) design or implementation for application X?  - what is property X of artifact/method Y?
- how does X compare to Y?    - What is the current state of X / practice of Y?
d. About Generalization or characterization over whole class of systems or techniques
- given X, what will Y (necessarily) be?     - what, exactly, do we mean by X?
- what are the important characteristics of X? - what is a good formal/empirical model for X?  - what are the varieties of X, how are they related?
e. About exploratory issues concerning existence or Feasibility
- feasibility Does X even exist, and if so what is it like?   - is it possible to accomplish X at all?


##### Software Engineering Research Results
**Types of Research Results** 
* **Procedure or Technique**: A new or better way to perform a task, such as design, implementation, or measurement.
* **Qualitative or Descriptive Model**: A structure or taxonomy for a problem area, or a framework for integrating other results.
* **Empirical Model**: An empirical predictive model based on observed data.
* **Analytic Model**: A structural model precise enough to support formal analysis or automatic manipulation.
* **Notation or Tool**: A formal language to support a technique or model, or an implemented tool that embodies a technique.
* **Specific Solution**: A solution to an application problem that shows use of software engineering principles.
* **Answer or Judgment**: A result of a specific analysis, evaluation, or comparison.
* **Report**: Interesting observations or rules of thumb.

##### Validation
Foundations for Acceptance of Research Results
The acceptance of any research results depends heavily on:
- the process of obtaining the results
- analysis of the results themselves
Research yields new knowledge. This knowledge is expressed in the form of a particular result.

***Types of Validity*** 
* **Statistical Conclusion Validity** `are the variables under study related?` 
	+ Examining the relationship between variables
	+ Threats include random heterogeneity and small sample size.
* **Construct Validity** `am I really measuring the construct that I want to study?` 
	+ Examining the measurement of the construct being studied
	+ "Construct" is an abstract idea or theoretical concept that can be inferred from one's behavior or performance. e.g. creativity
	+ Threats include subject apprehension(anxiety of subject), hypothesis guessing(participant guessing the hypothesis and trying to confirm or deny it), and experimenter bias
* **Internal Validity** - does A cause B? - done after we verify A and B are related.
	+ Examining causality between variables
	+ If a study is lacking internal validity, one cannot make cause and effect statements based on the research; the study would be descriptive but not causal.
* **External Validity** - would I find these same results with a difference sample, in a d/t setting..?
	+ Examining the ability to generalize results to other times, places, and persons
	+ Threats include inability to replicate results with different samples, settings, or times

##### Validation Techniques in Software Engineering
* **Experience**: The result has been used in actual use by someone other than the developer, with evidence of correctness, usefulness, or effectiveness.
* **Analysis**: The result is satisfactory through rigorous derivation and proof, data on controlled use, or experiment.
	* **Example**: The result is demonstrated with an example, typically on a system developed by the developer.
* **Persuasion**: A working system, even without analysis, can be persuasive for feasibility studies or pilot projects.
	*  . . . if you do it the following way . . .
* **Evaluation**: The result adequately describes, accounts for, or predicts phenomena of interest, and gives results that fit real data.
* **Blatant Assertion**: No serious attempt to evaluate the result is made.

# CH 5: Reviewing the Literature and Ethical Issues
### Literature review
- Is the an analysis, interpretation and synthesis of related works.
- It play a critical role in scholarship because science is a cumulative endeavor.

It is an important showcase of your talents of: understanding, interpretation, analysis, clarity of thought, synthesis, and development of argument.

Re + view = look again at what others have done in areas that are similar, though not necessarily identical to, one’s own area of investigation.

It helps to show researcher’s understanding and awareness of how the research fits into the overall field of study and how it will build on existing researches.

Reviewing the literature requires the ability to juggle multiple tasks:
- from finding and evaluating relevant material to synthesizing information from various sources,
- from critical thinking to paraphrasing, evaluating, and citation skills.

**"What is the purpose of a literature review?"** 
* Find out what information already exists in your field of research.
* Identify gaps in literature.
* Find other people working in your field.
* Identify major seminal works.
* Identify main methodologies and research techniques.
* Identify main ideas, conclusions and theories and establish similarities and differences.
* Provide a context for your own research.
* Show relationships between previous studies or theories.
- Reveal sources of data that you may not have known existed.

**Questions to be Explored in Review** 
1. Has the work already been done?
2. Who are the experts in the field?
3. What are the main theoretical perspectives?
4. What are the common research methods in the topic?
5. What are the main problems in researching the topic?
6. Are there any major controversies in this topic area?
7. Is the topic open to hypothesis testing?

##### Literature Review Steps
There are six generic steps involved in conducting a review.
1. **Formulate Research Question(s)**
	- Define the research question(s) proposed to be investigated. 
	- Justify the need for the review and identify the review’s main objective(s). 
	- Define the concepts or variables at the heart of their synthesis.
2. **Search the Literature** -**Literature Search Coverage**
	1. *Exhaustive vs. representative coverage:* 
		  * Exhaustive coverage includes all possible sources.
		  * Representative coverage focuses on key sources that represent the broader field.
	2. *Central or pivotal works:* 
		  * Key/foundational studies or publications that are central to the research area.
3. **Screen for Inclusion** - Evaluate and determine relevance, avoiding biases.
4. **Assess Scientific Quality** - Rigor of the research design and methods.
5. **Extract Data** - Gather applicable information from each primary study included in the sample and decide what is relevant to the problem of interest.
6. **Analyze Data** - Collate, summarize, aggregate, organize, and compare the evidence extracted from the included studies.
	- quantitative (e.g., frequency analysis, meta-analysis) and qualitative (e.g., grounded theory, narrative analysis, meta-ethnography) methods

##### Reviewing Guidelines
**Have a Plan** 
* This effort requires thought, structure, and coherence.
* Outline the plan before starting to write
* Discuss the problem from a comprehensive perspective, starting with broad areas and narrowing down to more specific ideas and studies
**Emphasize Relatedness** 
* Literature reviews should demonstrate the relatedness of cited studies to the problem being researched. Point out precisely what the relationship is.
* Each study should be explained in context of its relation to the current research problem.
* Citing studies without clear connection is not a discussion of related literature. 
	* not a chain of isolated summaries of the writings of others.
	* not, Jones says …; Black says ...; Brown says ...; Smith says …
**Give Credit where Credit is Due** 
* Always credit authors whose ideas you're using or whose research results you're reporting.
* Use specific citation methods, such as footnotes or in-text citations.
* Present other authors' words with quotation marks or indented passages.
**Don’t Reproduce the Literature** 
* Present your own discussion of the literature.
* Paraphrase or give a short, direct quotation if necessary.
* Long quotations should be avoided unless the specific words are essential.
**How to read a research paper** 
- Carefully read title, abstract, and introduction
- Read conclusions and section headings
- At the end, you should be able to answer:
	* Category: what type of paper is it
	* Context: to which papers is it related?
	* Correctness
	* Contributions
	* Clarity
- Read with greater care, but ignore details (e.g., proofs)

This is required to fully understand the paper, especially if you have to review  it.
1. Try to virtually re-implement the paper.
2. Make the same assumptions as the authors.
3. Re-create the work, re-prove the results, …
4. Compare the re-creation with the actual paper.
5. This requires great attention to detail.
6. Think how you would present a particular idea.
7. You can learn new techniques, and good presentation style.
8. Note down open problems and ideas for future work

##### Types of Literature reviews
1. **Narrative (Traditional) Review**: Describes existing research and how it informs a study. 
	* It is often subjective (unsystematic) and qualitative, which can lead to bias, but it is useful for *identifying gaps* to formulate research questions or hypotheses.
	* lacks explicit criteria for inclusion and can lead to biased interpretations or inferences.
2. **Descriptive/Mapping/State-of-the-Art Review**: Conducted periodically to describe current knowledge and disagreements. 
	- It uses a systematic, transparent procedure to analyze publication patterns and trends using frequency analysis to produce quantitative results.
	- includes searching, screening and classifying studies, to form a representative sample.
3. **Critical Review**: Provides a detailed, interpretive analysis of literature to evaluate the strengths, weaknesses, controversies, and inconsistencies in theories, methods, or results.
	- It uses appraisal instruments to assess research credibility and constructively inform future studies.
4. **Conceptual Review**: Groups articles by concepts, categories, or themes to provide a *snapshot* of the current understanding of a research topic and explore potential improvements in that understanding.
	- goal is to determine the extent to which a body of knowledge in a particular research topic reveals any interpretable pattern or trend with respect to pre-existing propositions, theories, methodologies or findings.
5. **Scoping Review**: An initial, comprehensive examination of an emergent topic, including grey literature, to map the size and nature of research, identify gaps, and propose a research agenda. 
	- It uses inclusion/exclusion criteria to focus the scope.
	- conducted to examine the extent, range and nature of research activities in a particular area, determine the value of undertaking a full systematic review
6. **Systematic Literature Review (SLR)**: Uses a structured, tool-supported, and predefined method to aggregate, appraise, and synthesize all empirical evidence that meets specific eligibility criteria. 
	- It is designed to answer specific research questions, explain inconsistencies, and determine the overall strength of evidence.
	- The use of explicit methods allows systematic reviews to:
		- aggregate a large body of research evidence,
		- assess whether effects or relationships are in the same direction and of the same general magnitude,
		- explain possible inconsistencies between study results, and
		- determine the strength of the overall evidence for every outcome of interest based on the quality of included studies and the general consistency among them.

# CH 6: Writing up research proposals
##### Ingredients of a Research Proposal
1. **The Research Topic or Title** 
	* Should be explanatory of the research problem/the study
	* Clear and understandable
	-  must state *Variables included and their relationship* in research proposal, **population of your study** 
2. **Introduction and Background of the Study** 
	* Brief overview of the general area
3. **Statement of the problem & its justification** 
	- Define and delimit the specific area of the proposed research
	- It should be so structure
	- It can be measured
4. **Objectives of the Study** 
	* General: broad objective/high level
	* Specific: list of objectives to achieve the general objective
5. **Statement of Hypothesis** 
	- states what is expected to occur if various conditions are evoked
	* Unambiguous and testable
	* Researcher should review related literature thoroughly before formulating hypotheses
6. **Scope/delimitation of the Study** 
	- Defining the research focus/concern
	- Limiting the research boundary: 
	- physical & Logical boundaries
7. **Methodology/Method/Procedure of the Study** 
	* Defining the ways/procedures to be followed
	* Target population
	* Research type
	* Data collection tools to be used
8. **Significance/Benefits of the Study** 
	* What are the importance of the study
	* Potential beneficiaries at different levels - individual, organizational, national
9. **Budget Schedule** 
	* Resources needed
	* Money required for each activity
10. **Time Schedule** 
	- Beginning time for each activity and/or study
	- Ending time for each activity and/or study

##### Writing the proposal
**Research Proposal**: Where you express your intentions and plans (Future)
- It should be Written in future tenses
**Research Report**: Where you present what you have done (past).
- It should be Written in past tenses.

**Common Mistakes in Proposal Writing** 
1. Failure to provide the proper context to frame the research question.
2. Failure to delimit the boundary conditions for your research.
3. Failure to cite landmark studies.
4. Failure to accurately present the theoretical and empirical contributions by other researchers.
5. Failure to stay focused on the research question.
6. Failure to develop a coherent and persuasive argument for the proposed research.
7. Too much detail on minor issues, but not enough detail on major issues.
8. Too much rambling -- going "all over the map" without a clear sense of direction. (The best proposals move forward with ease and grace like a seamless river.)
9. Too many citation lapses and incorrect references.
10. Failing to follow the IEEE/APA style and Slopping writing.
